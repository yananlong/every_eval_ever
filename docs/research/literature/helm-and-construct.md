## HC01: methodology and condition-bound findings

HELM defines each evaluation through a scenario, an adaptation procedure and metrics, treating models as black-box text interfaces rather than assuming access to activations or training data (§2, pp. 14–15). The original study evaluates 30 models from 16 families and 12 organizations, including 28 text models and two models specialized to code, across 16 core scenarios and 26 additional targeted scenarios (§§1, 5–6, Table 5). Seven core metric categories cover accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity and efficiency, with 98 of 112 scenario-by-desideratum pairs measured, or 87.5% (Table 4, p. 27). The paper reports prior core-scenario evaluation coverage of 17.9%, increasing to 96.0% under HELM (abstract and Figure 4), which is an evaluation-coverage statistic, not an estimate of validity or overall capability coverage.

The protocol generally uses five fixed in-context training examples for all evaluation instances, selected to cover common classes where relevant, with three runs differing in sampled exemplars (§7, pp. 45–47 and Appendix J.2). Five examples are a maximum rather than a universal delivered count, because context windows can require fewer examples, and separate multiple-choice scoring differs from joint prompting. The general evaluation cap is 1,000 instances, while some datasets are smaller, and §11.2 explicitly acknowledges that three seeds and partial test-set evaluation can make comparisons statistically insignificant (p. 82). RAFT uses held-out public training data because the full test labels are private (§3.8, p. 25). TruthfulQA reserves one fifth of its 817-question dataset for sampling five exemplars and evaluates on the remainder (Appendix B.1.8, p. 125). Results concern queried model versions, and the paper records timestamps while assuming live models do not change during evaluation (§6, pp. 44–45). Contamination is only partially documented, so the findings do not establish clean train–test separation for every model/scenario.

Accuracy is an umbrella for distinct task metrics, including exact match, question-answer F1, retrieval MRR/NDCG and summarization ROUGE, while calibration uses ten equal-mass bins and selective accuracy (§4 and Appendix C). Robustness is average worst-case performance across instance transformations, including the identity, which makes robust accuracy upper-bounded by ordinary accuracy (Appendix C.3, p. 133). The reported fairness metric similarly uses accuracy under demographic/dialect perturbations and worst-case accuracy, supplemented by subgroup performance when metadata exist (Appendix C.4). The near-diagonal accuracy/robustness/fairness relationships in Figure 24 therefore reflect particular related operationalizations. Social bias measures count-based demographic representation and stereotypical association, while toxicity uses Perspective API classifications, whose known validity limitations are explicitly retained. Treating these outputs as several interchangeable measures of one independently validated latent capability would exceed the paper.

## HC02: methodology, results and source-level limits

Kearns studies covariance structure and prediction within leaderboard BBH subtasks rather than auditing arbitrary benchmarks or measuring Every Eval Ever. The data begin with 4,576 OpenLLM Leaderboard v2 entries, and dropping 181 incomplete models yields 4,395 LLMs with 19 BBH subtask indicators (§3.2, pp. 21–23). Four of the original 23 BBH tasks are unavailable for all models, and logical deduction plus tracking shuffled objects are each recombined from three difficulty splits. Every recorded subtask uses multiple-choice scoring, selecting the highest-probability answer letter, with reported option counts ranging from two to nineteen. These are subtask mean-accuracy indicators, not question-level IRT fits or 19 independent model populations.

Experiment A compares four models with five factors: linear EFA, linear EFA with log parameter count, transformed-data EFA and transformed-data EFA with log parameter count (§3.3–3.4, Eqs. 13a–d). The transformation adapts a three-parameter logistic curve with a fixed chance-level floor to subtask means, assuming equally difficult items within a subtask for the derivation. Because `lavaan` cannot directly express the desired nonlinear response, the author transforms observed scores with the inverse curve and fits a linear structural equation model. AIC/BIC comparisons are reported within raw-data or transformed-data pairs, while the text correctly says likelihoods for raw and transformed data are not directly comparable. Five factors are chosen after parallel analysis suggests five for raw scores and a marginal sixth for size-adjusted residuals (§4.1, p. 33).

Experiment B leaves one BBH subtask out of capability estimation, fits five-factor structured capabilities or five-component PCA using the other eighteen subtasks, and uses the **bottom 80% of held-out-subtask scores** to fit a downstream regression, testing on the remaining high-scoring models (§3.4, pp. 29–30). This is not a random 80/20 split or a split by parameter size. PCA operates on raw scores, whereas the structural model operates on transformed scores, and downstream prediction targets transformed scores to implement the intended logistic relationship. Reported MSE therefore is not raw accuracy-scale MSE or a percentage-point error. Other subtask scores are available for the same models represented in the held-out target's test partition, so the experiment concerns cross-subtask/high-score extrapolation, not deployment to wholly unseen models or model families.

The thesis is useful as an exploratory sensitivity study, but several distinctions constrain interpretation. The capability factors receive interpretations from post hoc task clusters, and the thesis performs no confirmatory factor analysis, intervention or independent external-criterion validation (§5.4, p. 47). An association between a factor and parameter size can reflect substantive ability as well as a scale-linked nuisance, so the thesis's claim that an existing factor is “illusory” is an author interpretation rather than an experimentally identified mechanism. The source compares factor structure in raw linear EFA against structured transformed EFA even though transformed EFA without scale has a similar structure, and the claimed predictive gain is nonsignificant under the reported test. The thousands of leaderboard entries should also not automatically be treated as independent architectures or model families: the thesis reports 64 architectures in the full leaderboard and does not describe family-disjoint validation or a deduplication procedure comparable to the earlier Ilic/Gignac sample selection.

A reproducibility gap worth retaining in the evidence ledger concerns the inverse transformation. Equation 11 requires `c_i < B_i < 1` to yield a finite real log value, while the inspected scatterplots contain scores at or below chance and some near boundaries. The read methods and Appendix D do not explain clipping, smoothing or exclusion for these observations, nor establish that transformation parameters are fitted solely within each training fold. This is a specific unresolved preprocessing question rather than a demonstrated implementation error, because the released analysis code has not been rerun here. Likewise, the source's reported AIC/BIC gains should not be independently certified without checking whether likelihoods condition on the same observed variables when log size is added. Neither issue should be silently resolved by assumption.

The thesis's own limitations place the results within multiple-choice BBH tasks, whose artificial settings do not establish ecological validity or safety/alignment validity. Several rhetorical claims in the introduction and conclusion, including “first” valid capability model and generalized superior performance, are stronger than the demonstrated experimental comparisons, so a literature synthesis should foreground the operational design and report the mixed results rather than inherit those claims.

## Implications for ECCI and Every Eval Ever

HC01 supports retaining protocol and metric context when organizing evaluation evidence, making coverage and missingness explicit and treating aggregation as a decision requiring weights. HC02 supports treating latent-factor interpretation as an empirical modeling question, documenting the model population, transformations, residual structure and held-out prediction target. Neither source measures Every Eval Ever, validates ECCI, proves all benchmarks invalid or demonstrates that metadata alone makes heterogeneous scores comparable.

A useful design implication, explicitly an inference from these studies, is that a catalogue can make future comparison more defensible by preserving exact benchmark versions, adaptation methods, delivered exemplar counts, scoring rules, model versions and uncertainty information alongside reported scores. An ECCI-style construct mapping would additionally need operational definitions and validation evidence appropriate to each claim, including sensitivity to model population and preprocessing. Listing more evaluations and assigning more labels does not supply that evidence on its own.

### Synthesis

HELM shows why an evaluation catalogue should preserve the conditions under which a score was obtained, because the same model and benchmark can produce markedly different results across adaptation protocols and model rankings can change with the chosen protocol. In the original study, OPT (175B) achieved HellaSwag accuracies of 79.1%, 54.8% and 30.2% under separate, separate-calibrated and joint adaptation, with the separate and joint approaches also differing in zero-shot versus five-shot prompting, while the OpenBookQA comparisons changed substantially across those same approaches. HELM's broader scenario-by-metric matrix makes coverage, missing measurements and trade-offs visible, providing a precedent for contextualizing reported results before choosing aggregation weights (Liang et al., 2023, §8.2 and §11.3).

Construct interpretation adds a further empirical requirement, illustrated by Kearns's exploratory thesis on 4,395 OpenLLM Leaderboard models and nineteen multiple-choice BBH subtasks, which compares latent-factor, scale-informed and PCA-based representations using fit indices and held-out-subtask prediction. The structured model achieved lower average test MSE than the PCA-based comparator, 1.156 versus 1.473 on the transformed-score prediction task, but the reported difference was statistically nonsignificant, and the unstructured transformed factor model already resembled the structured factor decomposition. These results motivate testing how inferred capability structure depends on scale, transformations and validation design, with the thesis's evidence remaining specific to the observed BBH score distribution and its exploratory modeling choices (Kearns, 2026, §§3.4, 4.2–4.3 and 5.4).

For Every Eval Ever, the practical implication is to preserve evaluation provenance and claim scope so that later users can distinguish a reported score from the capability interpretation built around that score. Such records would provide inputs to comparability and construct-validation analyses, while the validity of an ECCI mapping would remain a hypothesis to test against explicit measurement models and relevant external evidence.
