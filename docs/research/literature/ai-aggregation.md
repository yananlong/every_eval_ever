# AI aggregation and latent capability evidence ledger

This reading ledger supports the [consolidated evidence index proposal](../consolidated-evidence-index.md).
Scope: AI benchmark aggregation, latent capability estimation and sparse evaluation. Access and search cutoff: **2026-10-07**. This is a bounded, source-oriented continuation of the literature search, not an exhaustive systematic review. It contains **four complete paper readings**, with every main results figure inspected visually and relevant appendices read, and separately records screened candidates that have not received that treatment.

The evidence supports treating latent capability estimation and prediction from a small evaluation subset as established methodological starting points, while reserving the proposed contribution for testing how provenance, repeated reporters, setup variation and admissible analysis choices change an archive-based synthesis. Predicting a model's full benchmark result from selected items is a different estimand from predicting a later report by a different evaluator; none of the four included papers validates the proposal's source reliability or evidence independence measures.

## AI01 — tinyBenchmarks: evaluating LLMs with fewer examples

**Publication/version.** Felipe Maia Polo et al., ICML 2024, PMLR 235:34303–34326. [Canonical record](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/maia-polo24a.html); [accepted 24-page PDF](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlresearch/v235/main/assets/maia-polo24a/maia-polo24a.pdf). Read all pages 1–24, including the consistency proposition, estimator derivation, data/split details and all result appendices. Local `tiny.pdf` SHA256: `9b226a13e97f1a2307ee557457952aed4d7ce8be163ae893a0b44c9ec72f8412`.

**Method and scope.** The target is a fixed full-benchmark score, estimated from a small subset of items for a new model, using item responses from previously evaluated models. The paper compares stratified random sampling, correctness-vector clustering and multidimensional item-response-theory clustering; cluster-mass weights represent the full item population. A multidimensional two-parameter logistic IRT model supplies difficulty and discrimination vectors and model ability, with dimension selected from 2,5,10,15. The generalized estimator mixes observed weighted accuracy with an IRT estimate, using an estimated bias/variance heuristic rather than a universally optimal finite-sample weight (§§3–4, pp. 3–5).

The estimator's consistency proposition assumes true item parameters are known, ability estimation is consistent and discrimination is uniformly bounded (§4.2, p. 5). Its weighting approximation treats the raw estimator as unbiased and the model estimator's variance as negligible; for anchors it uses an additional heuristic variance adjustment. Continuous scores are binarized to fit IRT and then used in the scoring construction (§4.3), so the binary model should not be described as a fully specified likelihood for arbitrary heterogeneous evaluation metrics.

**Data and validation.** Open LLM uses 395 models and six benchmark scenarios with about 29,000 items, MMLU uses 57 subjects and about 14,000 items, HELM Lite v1.0.0 supplies 30 complete models over 10 scenarios and about 10,000 items, and AlpacaEval2 supplies 100 models and 805 preference items (§5, p. 6; Appendix D). Splits include random 75/25, temporal model splits, organization-held-out HELM evaluation and cross-validation; these test model transfer under a shared evaluation representation, not held-out reporters. Forty domain-specialized models and an ANLI prompt-variation experiment test particular distribution shifts, with five repeats reported for the principal error analyses.

**Exact quantitative evidence.** Figure 1's legend reports mean error 0.019 and Spearman correlation 0.92 for predicting full MMLU using 100 items on a temporal held-out population; this is a mean 1.9-percentage-point error, not a maximum-error guarantee. Figure 4's printed 100-item legends report:

| Target | Random MAE / Spearman | IRT MAE / Spearman | IRT++ MAE / Spearman |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open LLM | .017 /.98 | .015 /.99 | .008 /.99 |
| MMLU | .042 /.80 | .027 /.91 | .019 /.92 |
| HELM | .007 / 1.00 | .007 /.99 | .008 /.99 |
| AlpacaEval2 | .022 /.98 | .015 /.97 | .013 /.97 |

These Figure 4 values describe the illustrated split; aggregate repeated analyses appear elsewhere. The paper's approximately 2% error result uses 100 items **per scenario**, giving 600 total Open LLM items and 1,000 HELM items rather than 100 total for those multi-scenario suites (§5, p. 7). Appendix E.2 reports random-sample requirements around 400 per Open LLM benchmark and greater than 400 for MMLU to match 100-item IRT++ performance, with substantial benchmark variation. Appendix B states that the released tinyMMLU anchor set selects the random seed with the best **test** performance (p. 14); its deployed set therefore needs fresh untouched evaluation rather than treating that selection criterion as independent validation.

**Visual reading ledger.** Inspected Figure 1 on p. 1; Figure 2 on p. 5; Figures 3–4 on pp. 7–8; Figures 5–8 on pp. 8–9; and Figures 9–32 throughout pp. 14–24. The general-error curves show improving accuracy with sample size but broad model variation, the specialized-model plots show correctness-cluster degradation under distribution shift, and the high global ranking correlations do not remove the close-pair ranking uncertainty visible in the appendix. The HELM example in Figure 4 also prevents a universal statement that IRT++ always improves every metric. Figure 2 visualizes a weighting heuristic, not an empirical validation result. Numerical values above come from printed legends, tables or text; no unlabeled curve coordinates were treated as exact measurements. Appendix Figure 9's normalized effective-sample-size comparison, 0.53 for correctness clustering and 0.85 for IRT clustering, concerns item sampling and must not be reused as an independence estimate for evaluation reports.

**Constraint on the proposal.** AI01 establishes a strong prediction baseline and supports structured item selection when the complete response matrix is available, but an archive with missing setup metadata and aggregate report scores may not supply those inputs. ECCI should compare its predictions against transparent means, family-balanced means and appropriately available latent/IRT baselines on exactly the same held-out reports, while retaining a separate source-dependence model. SACI should test whether the choice of anchors, item/scenario weighting and binarization changes conclusions, without claiming that this paper validates a specification multiverse.

## AI02 — Observational Scaling Laws and the Predictability of Language Model Performance

**Publication/version.** Yangjun Ruan, Chris J. Maddison and Tatsunori Hashimoto, NeurIPS 2024. [Canonical proceedings](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/1cded4f97cf5f01a284c574110b7e3b9-Abstract-Conference.html); [accepted 52-page PDF](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/1cded4f97cf5f01a284c574110b7e3b9-Paper-Conference.pdf). The canonical proceedings URL spells “Language” incorrectly in its title metadata; the human-readable title above follows the paper. Read pp. 1–52, including derivations, collection details, preprocessing, selection objective, all experiment appendices and checklist. Local `obs.pdf` SHA256: `0c5060f81d055abe28e326311c548ef8ad016704e1ec5cd1a18c7867686a7f5e`.

**Method and scope.** The authors model diverse benchmark scores through a small number of principal components, then predict a target task using a sigmoid of a linear combination of those components. They additionally fit family-specific linear relationships between compute and the component scores, enabling a derived Llama2-equivalent compute scale; it is a performance-linked transformation rather than an independent measurement of actual training compute (§§2–3; Appendix B). Input scores are mean-centered without variance scaling, fewer than 1% missing entries are imputed iteratively, and preprocessing is fitted on training models (§D.3, p. 24). The three-component representation reflects the chosen benchmark/model population and does not itself establish stable psychological constructs or causal mechanisms.

**Data and splits.** The base-model analysis covers 77 models in 21 families and eight benchmarks: MMLU, ARC-C, HellaSwag, Winogrande, TruthfulQA, GSM8K, XWinograd and HumanEval (§D.1). Forty-seven models at or below Llama2-7B training compute,8.4×10^22 FLOPs, form the main training set and 30 larger models are tested; arithmetic tasks use a lower cutoff and omit GSM8K from predictors to avoid direct target leakage. Open-model inputs use standardized evaluation, while proprietary scores are partly collected from heterogeneous public reports (§D.1.2), a material limit for an archive-level interpretation. The paper also evaluates AgentBench/AgentBoard, prompting effects and 20 subsequently released models in eight families between May and September 2024, described by the authors as a preregistered test; this lane did not independently audit the historical preregistration repository.

**Exact quantitative evidence and exceptions.** Figure 2 reports cumulative explained variance 0.967 for three principal components, with the first accounting for approximately 80%; the tuned-model Figure E.1 reports 0.986. Several within-family first-component/compute fits have R² above 0.9, but the low-order components do not obey the same regularity in every family: Figure E.2 labels Qwen1.5 PC2/PC3 R² 0.08/0.04 and Falcon 0.00/0.01. In Figure 4, printed held-out MSEs for compute versus PC prediction are 0.013 versus 0.004 for Word Unscramble, 0.021 versus 0.0032 for PersianQA, 0.063 versus 0.013 for three-digit subtraction, and 0.12 versus 0.0099 for two-digit multiplication. Those Figure 4 values include the subsequently added-model evaluation; Appendix Figure E.8 gives the earlier test separately.

The appendix supplies genuine exceptions to blanket superiority. Figure E.13's default arithmetic cutoff gives three-digit-subtraction MSE 0.0028 for FLOPs versus 0.031 for PCs, even though the surrounding prose says PCs still outperform compute. Figure E.16 gives BBH chain-of-thought MSE 0.0033 for FLOPs versus 0.0097 for PCs, while its naive-prompt setting favors PCs, 0.003 versus 0.0065. Figure E.11's PersianQA area-under-extrapolation comparison gives 0.0416 for PC3 versus 0.0191 for FLOPs, with PC1 at 0.00634. Figure E.14's IPA Transliterate comparison gives model-size MSE 0.092 versus PC 0.096; this does not support an unqualified best-predictor statement. Figure 5's agent-result labels report MSE 0.00022 for AgentBench and 0.0036 for AgentBoard, but these are task-level prediction errors and are not uncertainty-calibration or source-reliability measurements.

**Visual reading ledger.** Inspected main Figures 1–7 on pp. 2,4,5,7–10; Appendix Figures B.1–B.2 on p. 20; Figures E.1–E.22 on pp. 25–43, including E.12 on p. 34. Main plots distinguish fitted trends, training and test models, later releases and family symbols, while component loadings show the particular benchmark composition used to name capabilities. Appendix plots expose sensitivity to cutoff, prompting condition and number of components, so the main success plots should be read alongside those changes. All quoted MSE, R² and explained-variance values are printed labels or text rather than visual estimates. Contact sheets were inspected for trends and panel comparison, with text extraction used for labels; no unsupported precision was inferred from scatter positions.

**Constraint on the proposal.** This is direct prior art for a latent capability vector and held-out model-performance prediction, and it motivates evaluating dimensionality, normalization, imputation and training-population choices inside SACI. It also shows why low-dimensional fit or an impressive explained-variance percentage cannot justify a universal transferable score: prediction is target- and split-dependent, and several appendix settings reverse the claimed baseline ordering. Its use of heterogeneous reported scores does not estimate reporter biases or repeated-report correlation, so ECCI's source-aware layer remains an empirical question rather than a consequence of observational scaling.

## AI03 — From Benchmarks to Skills: A Low-Rank Framework for LLM Evaluation

**Publication/version.** Aviya Maimon, Amir David Nisan Cohen, Gal Vishne, Shauli Ravfogel and Reut Tsarfaty, TACL 2026, volume 14:1660–1684. [Canonical ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.tacl-1.75/); [accepted 25-page PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2026.tacl-1.75.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1162/TACL.a.745). PDF front matter records submission July 2025, revision January 2026 and publication July 2026. Some secondary indexes list different pagination; this ledger uses the accepted PDF and primary record. Read all pp. 1–25, including factor assumptions, new-task/model procedures and Appendices A–H. Local `lowrank.pdf` SHA256: `73987894ac5516815c03bc4469b1d41c1672a0caf4845f32e2cdc864b2409ea0`.

**Method and scope.** Principal-axis factor analysis decomposes the task correlation matrix into shared latent factors and task-specific uniqueness, with orthogonal factors, diagonal residual covariance and Varimax rotation (§3). The authors retain eight factors using the Kaiser eigenvalue criterion and an 85% cumulative-variance threshold, then name factors from strongly loading tasks with human-reviewed LLM assistance. The names are interpretations of a statistical decomposition, not independently identified causal abilities; orthogonality and rotation choices also matter for comparison with an ECCI vector.

**Data and validation.** The dense evaluation matrix covers 60 instruction-tuned models from 24 families and 44 tasks, comprising 25 classification tasks and 19 generation tasks, with three prompt formulations averaged (§4; Tables 1–3). Generation uses DeepSeek-R1 judging, and the detailed task table also uses a judge for some multiple-choice evaluations. Appendix E describes prompt refinement on 50 labeled examples across 10 models until at least 70% valid answers per model, with other models retained at a 50% validity threshold; this conditioning and task-specific judging differ from an uncurated archive of reported scores. Small model-sample and leave-one-task-out stability checks supplement model/task recovery experiments, but no held-out evaluator or repeated-report correlation analysis is supplied. The factor-labeling model names differ between §4.1 and Appendix C, a reproducibility detail that should be resolved before imitating the procedure.

**Exact quantitative evidence.** Section 5.1 reports all uniqueness estimates below approximately 0.40, maximum MMLU 0.38, and reliability measures Cronbach α 0.836 and McDonald ω 0.821 (pp. 6–7). Dimensionality changes to six or seven factors give maximum angle changes of 43–60°, whereas adding factors leaves core-factor angles at most 26°; subsampling 30% of models gives factor angles below 14° with standard deviations below 8°, and leave-one-task-out perturbations at most 2.4° (p. 7; Figures 6–8). These stability checks address this fitted matrix and perturbation protocol, not stability across the unrestricted universe of evaluation tasks.

Section 5.2 compares 13 overlapping models with Arena rankings: the mean of the eight factor scores has Spearman ρ 0.73, p<0.005; leave-one-out correlations average 0.722±0.058. Yet the simple task average correlates approximately 0.86 and HotpotQA FullWiki approximately 0.97 with Arena, exceeding the factor mean (§5.2, p. 9). The comparison supports an interpretable profile but does not establish that collapsing that profile into a scalar improves alignment over a transparent mean. For new-model recovery, Section 6.2 selects 12 high-communality tasks and reports mean factor-score MSE 0.095 in 20 held-out-model trials; for new-task recovery, Section 6.3 reports normalized MSE below 0.20 and mean Pearson correlation above 0.85 for well-represented tasks, with bAbI an example of poor prediction. Task-novelty thresholds in §6.1, including correlation 0.9, R² 0.8 and reconstruction MSE 0.10, form an author-designed diagnostic heuristic rather than externally validated semantic novelty criteria.

**Visual reading ledger.** Inspected Figure 1 on p. 2; Figure 2 on p. 5; Figure 3 on p. 7; Figures 4–5 on p. 21; Figures 6–8 on p. 22; Figure 9 on p. 23. The radar diagrams show different latent profiles for models with similar aggregate standing, while the loading heatmap displays mixed signs and cross-loadings that complicate a one-task/one-skill interpretation. The stability plots show material sensitivity to under-extraction and smaller changes for the tested over-extraction settings; cumulative variance near the eight-factor threshold is a selection rule, not proof of exactly eight intrinsic capabilities. The score heatmap's negative/positive coordinates are relative factor scores and should not be read as absolute incapability or a causal tradeoff. No plotted confidence interval was converted into an uncertainty estimate, and all quoted numerical results are text-reported rather than approximations from radars or heatmaps.

**Constraint on the proposal.** This is particularly close prior art for capability vectors, redundant evaluations, identifying minimally sufficient tasks and recovering missing profiles, so those elements cannot carry ECCI novelty by themselves. Its own Arena comparison requires the proposal to retain simple aggregation as a serious baseline rather than treating latent-factor sophistication as automatic improvement. A useful ECCI extension would test whether a provenance-aware model improves prediction and calibration on genuinely new reporters after controlling for setup, while SACI would expose factor number, rotation, normalization, task weighting and coverage restrictions as explicit analytical choices.

## AI04 — SparseEval: Efficient LLM Evaluation via Sparse Annotation

**Publication/version.** Taolin Zhang, Hang Guo, Wang Lu, Tao Dai, Shu-Tao Xia and Jindong Wang, ICLR 2026. [Canonical proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/b059de4629cccb411ef92d0d4157e87e-Abstract-Conference.html); [accepted 15-page PDF](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/file/b059de4629cccb411ef92d0d4157e87e-Paper-Conference.pdf); [arXiv 2602.07909](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07909). Read pp. 1–15, including definitions, Proposition 2 and Appendix C proof, all tables and implementation details. Local `sparse.pdf` SHA256: `4d24ec07a6ed2b35187c9ce5e6f8233dbdc18b1d2163ac09ac184234f08cf3e8`.

**Method and scope.** SparseEval predicts a full benchmark mean from selected item responses. Its training score matrix uses correctness encoded as−1/+1, while “sparsity” principally concerns redundancy in response vectors rather than missing archive cells (§3.1). It alternates proxy-model fitting and item replacement: anchor importance measures response-vector alignment with the residual, candidate importance searches for an informative replacement, and the final predictor is a four-layer MLP. The linear theoretical variant allows unconstrained real weights with support size at mostk; it is not a probability-weighted composite index, and its negative learned weights should not be transplanted into reporter credibility weights.

**Data and splits.** Experiments use approximately 5,000 Open LLM models on ARC, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA and Winogrande, with 200 models randomly assigned to validation/test,100 each, and the remainder to training (§4.1). Thirteen DeepSeek-family models provide a separate family-transfer analysis. Item budgets are 20,40,60,80 and 100, and the implementation uses 10 refinement steps with learning rate 6×10^-4. The paper references the leaderboard v2 while listing the older six-task suite; that naming should be checked against a reused dataset. The random model split is not a reporter split or time split, and family-related models in both training and test may permit easier interpolation than predicting a later archive report.

**Exact quantitative evidence.** Table 1, p. 7, prints the following 100-anchor results. MAE is in percentage points; τ is Kendall correlation.

| Benchmark | SparseEval MAE | Kendall τ |
|---|---:|---:|
| ARC | 1.165 | .917 |
| GSM8K | 1.619 | .936 |
| HellaSwag | .827 | .918 |
| MMLU | .842 | .908 |
| TruthfulQA | 1.027 | .931 |
| Winogrande | 1.027 | .897 |

The table contradicts an unqualified narrative that error is below 1% and τ exceeds 0.90 across all datasets: only HellaSwag and MMLU have MAE below 1 percentage point, and Winogrande has τ 0.897. Table 2 also shows settings where the initialization baseline wins: HellaSwag 100-anchor k-means has MAE 0.772/τ 0.919 versus SparseEval 0.827/0.918, and TruthfulQA 40-anchor k-means has 1.417/0.892 versus SparseEval 1.589/0.886. In the DeepSeek transfer table, average MAE is 2.090 versus gpIRT 2.657 and TailoredBench 4.216, with maximum errors 4.616,6.195 and 12.865 respectively; individual comparisons still reverse, such as DeepSeek-Math-7B-Instruct 3.810 for SparseEval versus gpIRT 1.950 (Table 3, p. 9). Figure 9 prints MMLU training time 954 seconds for IRT versus 48.2 seconds for SparseEval, but absent a transferable hardware/configuration comparison these numbers do not justify a general runtime guarantee.

**Visual reading ledger.** Inspected Figure 1 on p. 2; Figure 2 on p. 4; Figures 3–6 on p. 8; Figures 7–10 on p. 10; and Figure 11 on p. 14, plus Tables 1–3 on pp. 7–9. Item-response heatmaps and sorted similarity plots document redundancy in this population, anchor-budget curves favor SparseEval in many settings, and residual-alignment densities shift during optimization; none measures reporter independence or external construct validity. The architecture/training-size panels and DeepSeek errors show useful bounded evidence of transfer, with no confidence intervals or reported repeated random-split distribution sufficient for a general guarantee. Quoted results are printed table cells, text or labeled timing annotations; no exact values were read off unlabeled curves.

**Reviewer-generated contradiction to Proposition 2, separate from empirical findings.** The accepted version's Proposition 2 (pp. 6–7) claims that replacing the least-important current anchor with the most-important candidate, followed by re-optimization of weights on the new support, cannot increase the linear reconstruction error, and gives a strict-improvement condition based on greater candidate importance and independence. Appendix C's argument (pp. 13–14) assumes the current weights are not optimal but treats replacement as if it enlarged the available descent directions; equal-size, nonnested feature spaces do not have that property. The following exact real-valued score-matrix counterexample uses the paper's two-valued−1/+1 correctness encoding and fits its unconstrained linear support formulation; it is not a 0/1 binary matrix.

Let the four-row, three-column matrix have columns

\[
a=(1,1,1,1)^\top,\quad b=(1,1,1,-1)^\top,\quad c=(1,1,-1,1)^\top,
\]

and full-score target \(\mu=(a+b+c)/3=(1,1,1/3,1/3)^\top\). Take one anchor, current support \(A=\{a\}\), and nonoptimal current coefficient \(w_a=13/20\). The residual is

\[
r=w_a a-\mu=(-7/20,-7/20,19/60,19/60)^\top.
\]

The paper's absolute correlation scores give \(\mathrm{AIS}_a=|a^\top r|=1/15\) and \(\mathrm{CIS}_b=\mathrm{CIS}_c=7/10\). Thus either candidate maximizes CIS, exceeds AIS and is linearly independent of the removed column. Nevertheless re-optimizing squared Euclidean loss gives

\[
\min_t\|ta-\mu\|_2^2=4/9\quad(t=2/3),\qquad
\min_t\|tb-\mu\|_2^2=11/9\quad(t=1/2),
\]

with the same increase for replacing a by c. Under an absolute-error interpretation the minima likewise increase from 4/3 to 2. The proposition specifies no intercept, simplex or normalization constraint that would exclude this support/weight setup; adding an intercept would change the problem. This counterexample was independently numerically checked by a second reviewer, and it refutes the stated general monotonic linear swap claim. It does **not** refute the reported MLP experiments or prove that the empirical selection algorithm usually worsens results. For implementation, monotonicity can be imposed by retaining a swap only when its re-fitted, explicitly specified objective improves, rather than citing the proposition as a guarantee.

**Constraint on the proposal.** SparseEval is close prior art for efficient prediction from redundant evaluation items and another useful baseline where EEE has item responses. It cannot establish archive reporter reliability, and the theoretical counterexample makes it particularly unsuitable as support for an unconditional guarantee that pruning or replacing correlated evaluations improves a composite index. The relevant transferable principle is to validate the exact prediction target on untouched data and distinguish the optimized in-sample residual objective from held-out ranking, calibration and reporter transfer.

## Findings that change or defeat potential claims

| Potential claim | Source-grounded consequence |
|---|---|
| A latent capability vector or prediction from a small benchmark subset is itself new | Defeated by AI01–AI03; AI03 is especially close to vector profiles, redundancy and minimal-task recovery. |
| A factor-based scalar is necessarily superior to transparent means | Contradicted by AI03's Arena comparison, where the simple task mean exceeds the factor mean. |
| Observational PC prediction universally beats compute | Contradicted by specific printed appendix results in AI02; superiority must name target, cutoff and prompt condition. |
| SparseEval guarantees every greedy replacement improves the unconstrained linear objective | Refuted by the exact reviewer-generated counterexample to accepted Proposition 2; empirical MLP results remain a separate question. |
| SparseEval is below 1% error with Kendall τ>.90 on every reported 100-item benchmark | Contradicted by Table 1 in AI04. |
| Released tinyMMLU is selected wholly without test information | Contradicted by AI01 Appendix B's best-test-seed selection; evaluate the released subset on fresh data. |
| High benchmark prediction accuracy demonstrates reliable reporters or independent evidence | Unsupported by all four included studies; item/model transfer is not reporter/source transfer. |
| High explained variance or stable loading signs establish general, causally identified capabilities | Unsupported by these methods and their assumptions; interpret factors within population, task set, rotation and measurement model. |

The proposed archive-level contribution remains viable if it is framed as an empirical test of provenance-aware synthesis and decision stability, supported by calibrated predictions across distinct reporting conditions. These papers make that validation burden sharper: the project needs to beat transparent and latent baselines on its own archive, publish reversals and failure regions, and preserve uncertainty about source effects when overlap cannot identify them.
