## Method, observations and validation

The method assigns task instances an explicit demand profile and estimates an individual model's ability profile from the model's responses to an annotated battery, providing a direct predecessor for common capability scales across changing benchmark collections. DeLeAn v1.0 contains 18 demand rubrics, comprising 11 elemental cognitive dimensions, five knowledge dimensions and two extraneous dimensions, Atypicality and Volume, with integer annotations from 0 to 5 and the upper category interpreted as 5+. Unguessability is an additional control variable on a 0–100 scale, giving the demand-based predictor 19 inputs, while the ability profile has 18 components. Unguessability describes error under obvious cues or random guesses, Atypicality describes familiarity or likely prior exposure, and Volume describes the competent human time needed to complete an item, so these variables should retain their distinct semantics rather than be described interchangeably as cognitive ability (Methods, “General scales”; Extended Data Table 5; supplementary §2 introduction).

The rubric examples specify the least demanding adequate solution, which can differ sharply from the sophistication of the task's wording or subject matter. For example, the spatial rubric assigns the regular-2019-gon diagonal calculation low spatial demand despite high logical demand, and the verbal-expression rubric generally assigns multiple-choice answering level 1 even when the options are complex. The academic, natural-science, formal-science and social-science knowledge rubrics anchor levels 1–5 to elementary, middle-school, high-school, undergraduate and graduate knowledge; customary/everyday knowledge instead uses familiarity across social and cultural contexts. Volume uses the competent autonomous human's reading and completion time under ideal conditions: less than one second, one second to one minute, 1–10 minutes, 10–100 minutes, 100–1,000 minutes and more than 1,000 minutes for levels 0–5+. These logarithmic time intervals reinforce the need to distinguish raw rubric categories from empirically calibrated common capability units (§2, printed pp.58–96).

Unguessability has a separate answer-format classification prompt that explicitly supplies both the **question and reference answer**. It recognizes explicit options and implicit familiar answer sets, such as seven days or twelve months, and otherwise labels an item open-ended. Thus annotation and assessor inputs require more than an aggregate benchmark score, and an implementation using question text alone would omit an input supplied by the published protocol (§2, printed p.97).

The battery contains 16,108 instances from 63 tasks, grouped into 20 benchmarks originating in nine papers, and the 18 main demand dimensions yield 289,944 annotations. Curation reviewed 2024 proceedings at ICML, NeurIPS, ICLR, ACL, EMNLP and NAACL using titles containing “bench”, adding material from other venues and requiring availability by 26 December 2024. Selection excluded benchmarks where state-of-the-art systems such as GPT-4 already exceeded 75% accuracy, required objective, automatically verifiable questions and redistribution rights, and excluded explicitly identified AI-generated material and multiple-choice questions with fewer than four options. Up to 500 instances were sampled per task, giving 21,996 initial instances. GPT-4o quality screening removed items rated 1 on accuracy, objectivity or unambiguity, leaving 18,462, and missing or malformed annotations reduced this to 18,291. A final filter removed approximately 12% when GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet graders did not confidently agree on the correctness of **GPT-4o's response**, leaving the final battery. Both graders had to rate that response at least 4 or both at most 2 on a 1–5 scale. A 100-pair manual grading audit found 98% correct verification, while the earlier audit of 50 low-quality items per indicator included substantial uncertainty, especially 62% uncertainty for supposed ground-truth inaccuracies (Methods, “Benchmark battery: instance selection and curation” and “Subject LLMs and grading”).

Subject systems were six OpenAI models, five LLaMA variants and four DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-Qwen variants, spanning overall battery accuracy from Babbage-002's 0.102 to OpenAI o1's 0.843. Model configuration matters: o1 used low reasoning effort, ordinary models generally used temperature 0 and a 2,000-token output cap, o1 models used temperature 1, DeepSeek distilled models temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95, and reasoning models had 16,384 output tokens. Chain-of-thought prompting was supplied to non-reasoning models. The LLaMA 11B and 90B models were originally Vision variants evaluated only through text. These are comparisons of the configured systems and their inference budgets, with model size, training and reasoning setup changing together.

GPT-4o `gpt-4o-0513` annotated each rubric separately using temperature 0, chain-of-thought prompting and a 1,000-token response limit. Human validation sampled 50 instances per demand, with at least three from each observed level, yielding 900 annotation assignments distributed among five authors and three raters per instance. Ratings differing by at least two points were resolved in a Delphi discussion and smaller differences by majority vote. Agreement is reported as rWG under a rectangular null distribution, with average human agreement 0.83 before consensus and Delphi–GPT-4o agreement 0.86, ranging from 0.75 to 0.94. This evaluates a rubric's reproducibility against author consensus and is a separate question from empirical scale spacing, human-population calibration or causal interpretation (Methods, “LLM annotators and inter-rater analysis”; supplementary §1.15, Table 22).

Ability curves use **dominant slices**: an item contributes to a dimension at demand level l only when no other demand exceeds l. There are no all-zero control items, and some dimension-level combinations have very few observations. A two-parameter decreasing logistic curve is fitted with demand bins weighted equally except bins below 100 instances, which receive proportionally smaller weight, and an artificial point at demand 20 with success 0 receives **50% of the total fit weight**. The empirical demands end at 5, while displayed curves extend to 10 and stronger estimated abilities exceed 5. The source defines ability as the area under the curve from zero onward, describing this as approximately equal to the 50% crossing when success at zero is close to one, which requires care for weak subjects whose fitted zero-demand success is low (Fig. 3 caption; Methods, “Subject characteristic curves”; supplementary §1.2, pp.10–11 and footnotes 3–4).

Predictive validation trains an assessor separately for each subject model from instance demand annotations and that model's observed success labels. The main assessor is a random forest with minimum samples per split selected from 2, 50 and 200 using in-distribution data, compared with another random forest on average GloVe embeddings and a fine-tuned LLaMA-3.1-8B classifier on the question text. The latter uses NF4 quantization, bfloat16, LoRA, three epochs, batch size 16, weight decay 0.01 and learning rates selected from 10^-4 and 2×10^-5. Results use tenfold cross-validation repeated across ten seeds, with in-distribution instances, entire tasks withheld and entire benchmarks withheld as increasingly demanding splits, and calibration measured with ten equal-width probability bins. These evaluate prediction for additional items and task groups of an already observed model, while a universal-assessor ablation adds subject identity to a combined dataset and does not establish generalization to unseen model families (Methods, “Assessors and metrics”; supplementary §1.7.6).

The extraneous-only ablation shows that substantial predictivity can come from features of familiarity, work volume and answer format, while removing those inputs leaves appreciable predictive power in the cognitive and knowledge dimensions. This supports an analysis of construct mixtures but does not directly quantify the fraction of predictive variance uniquely attributable to each construct, because the dimensions correlate. Alternative annotator rWG averages are 0.83 for DeepSeek-V3 and 0.74 for LLaMA-3.1-8B, and the annotation swap retains substantial predictive transfer while still using the same selected battery and success labels. A 200-item multiple-choice reorder test gives exact reported average agreement 0.916, rounded to 0.92 in supplementary Table 19 (§1.11).

The formulaic assessor compares subject abilities with demands using logistic transformations and combines the 18 probabilities plus the normalized guessing quantity with a generalized mean. Supplementary Table 7 reports weighted AUROC/ECE of 0.738/0.206 for the geometric mean, 0.757/0.106 for r=0.25 and 0.741/0.137 for the arithmetic mean. These are separate results with substantially worse calibration than the fitted RF, so the superior RF results should not be attributed to a direct ability-minus-demand formula. Generic-difficulty and alternative-taxonomy baselines in Table 14 can have ID ECE as low as 0.001 while AUROC is 0.729 or lower, illustrating why calibration and discrimination must be reported together. The supplement's Tables 15 and 16 appear to interchange “benchmark OOD” and “task OOD” caption labels relative to their cited Extended Data Tables 3 and 4, so the canonical main-table split mapping above should be retained.

The alternative-taxonomy prompts also differ in granularity: Miller and Tang's six categories are binary present/absent labels, Bloom's taxonomy is a six-level ordered objective scale, and generic difficulty is rated 1–5. The ablation therefore compares particular annotation representations, rather than isolating the effect of taxonomy identity while holding label richness constant (§3, printed pp.98–101; Tables 14–16).

ADeLe-Light reduces redundancy using k=10 nearest neighbours in demand space and average squared distance below 0.21, randomly discarding 90% of marked low-variability items and retaining 6,179 instances, approximately 38% of the full battery (§1.3). The article reports roughly 4 seconds for demand-RF cross-validation on an M3 Pro CPU, versus 160 seconds for embedding RF and 300 GPU hours on a V100 for fine-tuning. These are model-fitting comparisons after demand annotation and subject evaluation, whose one-time and recurring costs are discussed separately in supplementary §1.18, and should not be quoted as total end-to-end evaluation speedups.

## Limits and design implications

The closest overlap with a linked capability-scale proposal is the explicit use of standardized item demands to place benchmarks and models in a common capability space, together with individual-model estimation, benchmark-coverage diagnosis and transfer tests. Applying this approach requires item content, demand annotations and model–item responses, while a repository of heterogeneous aggregate scores has a different observable structure. The paper therefore supplies a strong conceptual comparator and a possible item-data validation setting, with feasibility depending on access to those inputs.

The ratio-scale and population-independence arguments need precise scope. Methods “Ratio scales” acknowledges that the actual rubrics produce ordinal integer values and motivate common spacing using a human-rarity rule of thumb, roughly one solver per 10^(l−1), proposing a 95% chance of at least one success in a sample of 10^l people and explicitly leaving proper calibration to future work. This does not empirically establish that level 4 is exactly twice level 2, that every dimension shares the same unit, or that ordinal annotations have a validated ratio interpretation. Adding or removing other subject systems leaves an individual fit unchanged by construction, while modifying the battery's conditional demand mixtures can still alter a fit. Supplementary §1.1, printed p.9, explicitly acknowledges that dominant slicing induces dependence on the battery's demand correlations and can be poorly calibrated when items are less pure, which narrows the broad main-text claim of benchmark-distribution independence.

The upper tail is supported by a modelling choice and a heavy artificial anchor, with the source itself acknowledging few high-quality level-5 items. A displayed open scale can be extended, but repeated empirical measurement beyond the current annotation range, anchor-sensitivity checks and stability under newly added items would be needed to establish long-term nonsaturation and backward compatibility. For curves whose zero-demand success is well below one, area and 50% crossing also require separate treatment, because the supplement's stated near-equivalence condition does not hold uniformly.

Selection for clear and confidently gradable items constrains the reported predictivity, with an especially relevant dependency created by GPT-4o-based item screening, GPT-4o demand annotation and GPT-4o responses used to determine which items survive grader disagreement. Alternative annotators retain predictive quality and GPT-4o remains the most predictable subject, providing useful robustness evidence, while the grading filter and final outcome labels remain shared. The signed mean annotation-discrepancy correlation of −0.06 in supplementary Table 17 also masks dimension-specific coefficients from −0.35 to +0.38. These tests reduce some concerns but do not provide an independent outcome assessment of every demand dimension or remove selection dependence.

One rubric-to-annotation consistency issue warrants checking before reproducing the scales. The Atypicality level-5+ definition says tasks found in standard tests or benchmarks should receive less than level 5 (§2, printed p.96), whereas the main and supplementary characteristic plots include an AT level-5 dominant bin labelled n=60. This does not establish which individual annotation is wrong, but the operational handling of benchmark-origin items should be reconciled with the published rubric. The observed family-dependent curve counts likewise need an explicit response-inclusion rule when comparing subjects.

The comparison between abilities and demands yields interpretable predictive explanations, while the paper's stronger causal and counterfactual wording requires interventions or identification assumptions that these observational fits do not establish. Dominant slicing prevents a *larger* annotated demand from appearing alongside a target demand but allows several equal high demands and conditional mixtures of lesser demands, so a change in one fitted curve cannot automatically be attributed to a change in that capability alone. Similarly, strong benchmark-held-out prediction tests transfer within the selected battery and evaluated model pool, with deployment prediction, unseen-model transfer, independent human annotation, temporal rubric revision and aggregate-score linking remaining separate validation targets.
