# Temporal scale linking: full-text evidence review

This reading ledger supports the [consolidated evidence index proposal](../consolidated-evidence-index.md).
Review date and search cutoff: 2026-10-07. Five selected sources were read in full, including references and available appendices. The first four cover practical equating, population-invariance diagnostics, item-level evolving AI evaluation, and benchmark refresh; the fifth was added explicitly after citation expansion because aggregate shared-model linking is the closest prior art to DLCI. This is a bounded targeted review, not an exhaustive systematic review. Numerical results below are source results, not EEE results or validated DLCI thresholds.

## SL01 — Equating is stronger than a predictive link

The book develops equipercentile and linear equating under equivalent-group, common-person/single-group, and common-item designs. Its target population is part of the estimand: an equating function is not guaranteed to work for every individual or every possible group (printed pp. 7–8). Equating is symmetric/invertible; regression prediction can shrink toward the population mean and is asymmetric (pp. 10–12). Different skills call for concordance or more general linking; different levels of related skills involve vertical scaling, not automatically interchangeable scores.

For DLCI, the relevant analogy is a common-person design: the same frozen models evaluated on old and new portfolios resemble shared test takers (pp. 25–26). A shared model is not an internal common-item anchor. Its weights, serving endpoint, prompt, scaffold, inference budget and scoring configuration must be stable, or the same name no longer denotes the same respondent. In a common-item design, by contrast, unchanged items carry the bridge through different respondent populations (pp. 28–35). These designs have different identifying assumptions.

The book explicitly warns that a strong test–anchor correlation within groups cannot test the critical between-group invariance assumption (p. 32). Content representativeness can matter more than maximizing correlation (pp. 35–37). The practical recommendation of at least 20 anchor items belongs to educational-test construction; it does not establish a minimum number of AI bridge models. Repeated links can accumulate same-direction error; comparing two paths, or directly linking a late form back to an early form, diagnoses scale drift (pp. 37–39). The standard error of equating depends on score location and is larger in sparsely populated tails (p. 49), so a global uncertainty constant is inadequate.

There is no new empirical result to transfer from this instructional book. Its numerical examples and summaries of earlier studies are teaching illustrations. For example, pp. 18–20 discuss a 39-item test and subsamples of 468 and 702 from 8,426 test takers; these are not DLCI validation samples. Tucker and poststratification examples later in the book explain failures under unequal populations, weak anchors and attenuation, rather than establishing universally preferred procedures (pp. 59–67). Scoring-standard changes can be investigated by rescoring the same saved responses (pp. 41–44), motivating independent versions for questions, graders and model configurations.

**Visually inspected figures:** printed Fig. 1, p. 11 (physical 20), contrasts one-way prediction arrows with reversible equating arrows; it has no numeric axes. Fig. 3, p. 16 (physical 25), uses new-form versus reference-form scores to show different slopes for stronger and weaker groups, a schematic warning about population dependence. Fig. 9, p. 30 (physical 39), plots item percent correct on the new and reference forms, both 0–100; one item deviates strongly despite an otherwise ascending cloud, showing why overall correlation does not establish item invariance. Figs. 10–11, pp. 38–39 (physical 47–48), show separate equating strains and a form connecting two paths; these are graph schematics, not measured confidence bounds.

**DLCI consequence:** call the proposed quantity a linked index, state the target model population and domain, and reserve “equated/interchangeable” for a stronger demonstrated claim. Require independent overlap responses and stable evaluation configurations. Use local error profiles and path checks; do not copy a textbook anchor-item count into a bridge-model gate.

## SL02 — Population invariance must be tested rather than imposed

This study examines linear equating in a nonequivalent-groups-with-anchor-test design: X and anchor V are observed in population P, Y and V in Q, and a synthetic target population is a weighted mixture. Tucker, Levine observed-score and chained linear procedures assume different invariances involving regression, true scores or chained relationships (printed pp. 3–10). The paper measures disagreement between subgroup-specific and whole-population equating functions, standardized by the score standard deviation; its RMSD is a diagnostic of population sensitivity, not a confidence interval. Parallel linking imposes a shared slope, so it cannot diagnose the slope heterogeneity it removes.

The empirical data are AP English Language and Composition multiple-choice forms from 1998 and 2000 (p. 11): n = 79,434 and 112,868, respectively; the common embedded anchor has 14 items and reliability.82 in each year. Sex counts are 30,217 male and 49,217 female in 1998, and 42,317 male and 70,551 female in 2000. Table 1 reports X mean 32.8, SD 10.83, 56 items; 1998 anchor mean 9.258, SD 3.16; 2000 anchor mean 8.996, SD 3.22; Y mean 35.51, SD 11.39, 55 items. The surrounding text reverses the 55/56-item assignments, so the table and body should not be silently harmonized. Table 2 describes male/female anchor differences as 7.7% of an SD in each year and a between-year overall difference of 8.2% of an SD; these are standardized differences, not accuracy percentages.

For the main comparison, RMSD values range.005–.043 (p. 12, Fig. 1); parallel-linking RMSDs are.0185 for Tucker,.0183 for chained linear, and.0185 for Levine (p. 13). Pseudo-NEAT diagnostics self-equate a form through the anchor, where the identity function is expected. These sometimes distinguish methods more clearly and vary across year, but a within-population identity check does not prove generalization to another population (pp. 8–10, 19). The paper’s conclusion favors Levine in this dataset’s pseudo-NEAT checks, not as an unconditional rule for AI evaluation.

**Visually inspected figures:** Figs. 1–6, printed pp. 13–18 (physical 19–24), and Eq. 10 on printed p. 8 (physical 14). Fig. 1 uses raw score on x and standardized RMSD on y; near-overlapping method curves are larger at lower scores and approach zero at high scores. Fig. 2 plots subgroup-versus-total equating differences in raw-score units, positive for the male comparison and negative for the female comparison. These flat lines result from the parallel restriction. Fig. 3’s 1998 pseudo-NEAT curves put Tucker above Levine; Fig. 4’s 2000 curves have different shapes, including a V-shaped Levine curve and a nearly flat Tucker curve. Figs. 5–6 reproduce nonlinear diagnostic curves with more waviness and edge sensitivity. No plotted curves are sampling confidence bands. Graph readings are qualitative, and the exact numeric results cited above come from printed text.

**Reproduction caution:** Eq. 10 visibly prints a minus sign between weighted squared subgroup deviations under the square root. That is not a safe general RMSD implementation. Use a separately verified formula or obtain author clarification; do not transcribe the apparent typo into DLCI. The clean general RMSD definition in Eq. 8 is a safer conceptual reference.

**DLCI consequence:** assess whether links change by model family, capability range, source/setup and temporal cohort; use flexible subgroup comparisons alongside any constrained common fit. Correlation and a pooled regression are insufficient. Do not adopt.043 or another observed discrepancy as a universal acceptance threshold; calibrate tolerances against a declared decision consequence and held-out overlap.

## SL03 — Fixed item parameters enable extension, conditional on response data and model fit

The method uses a five-dimensional multidimensional 2PL IRT model, with item response probability logistic(a_iᵀθ + d_i). Reference models supply item-level responses on new items and prior anchor questions. Historical anchor parameters are held fixed, new item parameters are calibrated, and K-means over item representations selects representative anchors. An incoming model answers the accumulated anchors; its inferred ability predicts the remaining items, with observed-anchor and predicted-remainder contributions combined. This is an active item-level evaluation design, not a passive link between published benchmark averages (§§2–4; Appendix C).

The empirical suites contain 395 Open LLM Leaderboard models across six datasets and 428 MMLU models across 57 subjects (Table 1, p. 5). Open LLM item counts are ARC Challenge 1,212; GSM8K 1,359; HellaSwag 10,082; MMLU 14,042; TruthfulQA 857; WinoGrande 1,307. MMLU subjects range from 100 to 1,534 items. Random splits use 75% reference/25% test models; time splits use earlier versus later submission dates; family splits hold out Llama and Mistral. Twelve Open LLM chains and 20 MMLU chains simulate sequential additions using existing response matrices. Reported 95% intervals are across chains (§4, p. 5), not confidence that unknown future constructs remain invariant.

Exact Table 3 results (p. 8), Spearman ρ with full-evaluation rankings:

| Suite; anchor count | Random | Concurrent calibration | Fixed parameter calibration |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Open LLM; 25 |.82 |.89 |.88 |
| Open LLM; 100 |.91 |.94 |.94 |
| Open LLM; 200 |.96 |.97 |.97 |
| MMLU; 10 |.68 |.73 |.72 |
| MMLU; 25 |.85 |.83 |.84 |
| MMLU; 50 |.91 |.90 |.90 |

Thus the abstract’s high ranking fidelity is budget-dependent. The reported 2–3 percentage-point accuracy errors at 100 anchors do not describe every suite, family holdout or targeted domain shift. Figure 2’s constant question cost concerns the incremental calibration of old reference models on each new dataset. New models must still answer accumulated anchors, whose total grows. Appendix C implements stochastic variational inference in py-irt, with hierarchical item priors, ELBO optimization, Adam learning rate.1 and decay.9999; initial and concurrent calibration run 2,000 epochs and fixed-parameter steps 1,000. Main §3 describes maximum-likelihood calibration; that terminology does not fully match the appendix’s estimation description and should be checked before exact reproduction.

**Visually inspected figures:** Figs. 2–6 on pp. 6–9; Figs. 8–11 on pp. 15–16. Fig. 2 has chain step on x, MAE (%) on one axis and questions on the other; fixed calibration cost stays at 100 per addition while concurrent cost rises toward 600. Fig. 3 shows Open LLM and MMLU error curves across chain length and anchor budgets; the fixed and concurrent methods are close, while random sampling improves as the budget grows. The MMLU low-budget panels are visibly well above the universal “2–3%” interpretation. Fig. 4’s family holdout has a fixed-calibration spike around chain step 4, approximately 6–7 percentage points with a wide interval, and at that step performs worse than random sampling. These are visual approximations, not tabulated values. Fig. 5’s targeted MMLU shifts show STEM and mathematical settings around 8–10 percentage-point error. Fig. 6 shows Open LLM instability at 25 reference models and better stability with larger pools; it does not validate 100 as a universal bridge-model minimum. Fig. 8’s item maps show top-discrimination anchors concentrated in a narrow region while K-means anchors cover more of the plotted item space. The scalar plotting projection should not be mistaken for proof that the five-dimensional construct is invariant. Figs. 9–11 reinforce the poor top-discrimination strategy and the larger errors under some holdouts.

**DLCI consequence:** use this source to justify testing sequential calibration and representative anchors when EEE obtains response-level data. It does not license IRT calibration from aggregate scores or imply that frozen item parameters establish an unchanged construct. For aggregate DLCI, benchmark version and setup are part of the observation unit; new shared-model scores must exist rather than be inferred from a benchmark name.

## SL04 — Refresh can preserve ranks while changing score levels

LiveBench’s accepted version evaluates 40 models on 1,000 questions, 18 tasks and six categories. Questions use objective scoring where practical; some outcomes are partial-credit quantities rather than binary responses. For example, table joining uses F1, instruction following combines prompt-level and instruction-level accuracy, and some tasks use edit-distance or group-recovery scoring. Scores are aggregated questions → tasks → categories → equal mean across six categories; equal category weights do not mean equal weights for every question (§2; Appendix A). A binary item-response model therefore needs an explicit outcome adaptation rather than direct application to all LiveBench scores.

The design refreshes roughly one sixth of questions monthly, keeps new questions private for a month, and replaces the full set over approximately six months. It also changes task coverage and difficulty, including replacing old/easy material (§2.7; Appendix A.6). One reported update adds 50 spatial, 28 code-generation and 12 completion questions; a later update replaces 132 questions while maintaining 1,000 in total. Plans to cap routine evaluation at 40–50 popular models mean overlap is selected rather than a random model sample.

The strongest temporal evidence is §3.4: both adjacent-release rank correlations exceed.997, while mean and median scores of shared models fall by around 1.2% from the original to the most recent release. The paper’s wording is retained: it does not unambiguously label that “1.2%” as relative percent versus percentage points. Stable ranks and changing score levels coexist; the result is not an equating study or proof of invariant measurement. §3.3 reports LiveBench score correlations.91 with Chatbot Arena and.88 with Arena-Hard, but model/domain outliers remain.

Table 1 lists o1-preview 64.7, Claude 3.5 Sonnet October 58.5, Claude June 58.2, and o1-mini 56.7. The nearby prose ordering is stale relative to the table; do not repeat it. Appendix A.2 separately studies an LLM judge on reasoning answers. Table 8 reports judge error rates for GPT-4-Turbo’s own versus Claude Opus responses, respectively: AMC.380/.388, AIME.214/.103, SMC.353/.294, Zebra.420/.460. Those task-specific rates are not a universal judge error estimate. Parser improvements can require rescoring all models (Appendix A.5), so scoring software is an independent versioned measurement component. Appendix A.7 allows residual contamination and rejects an absolute contamination-free interpretation.

**Visually inspected figures:** Fig. 1, p. 2; Figs. 2–3, p. 8; Fig. 4, p. 9; Figs. 5–6, p. 17. Fig. 1 shows overlapping bootstrap intervals on many model bars and different six-category radial profiles. Fig. 2’s correlation heatmaps show substantial positive associations but weaker instruction-following relations; they do not test stability across refreshes. Fig. 3 plots category scores against the overall score with family outliers. Because the overall score includes each category, this is partly a part–whole correlation. Fig. 4 uses two different benchmark axes, so apparent bar distances are not interchangeable units. Fig. 6’s scatterplots against Arena scores show outliers around fitted lines; the authors’ discussion of response style is suggestive rather than causal identification.

**DLCI consequence:** LiveBench is evidence that continuous refresh and stable ranking are feasible in a particular operational benchmark. It also directly motivates separating release-specific ranks from linked absolute levels. Store question release, category/task weights, parser/grader version and model setup. Stable rank correlation is a necessary descriptive check in some applications, not a sufficient temporal-scale gate.

## SL05 — Direct aggregate linking prior art, with important fit and identification limits

Rosetta Stone is the closest source because its observation is a model’s aggregate score on a benchmark. It fits performance(m,b) = sigmoid(α_b(C_m − D_b)), where each model has one capability C, each benchmark a difficulty D and slope α (§2, pp. 2–3). It uses 179 models, 38 benchmarks and 1,324 observed scores, with coverage filtering removing models evaluated on three or fewer benchmarks. The fit is L2-regularized least squares, regularization.1, using SciPy’s trust-region reflective algorithm and initial C,D = 0 and α = 1. Fixing WinoGrande difficulty at 0 and slope at 1 selects the latent scale’s location and unit. It is a gauge choice, not an invariant anchor-item design. Aggregate scores from internal harness runs and external sources are combined; Appendix B footnote 20 takes the maximum where multiple evaluation settings exist. Such choices make evaluation setting and reporting selection material to interpreting C.

The method explicitly assumes one-dimensional capabilities. It is closer to the proposed shared-model DLCI than item-level psychometrics, so a claim that linking published benchmarks onto a latent scale is novel would be false. Its limitations (§4) also identify plausible improvements: evaluation-setting heterogeneity, correlated benchmarks/double counting, scarce overlap, saturation, poor real-world coverage, and domain specialization. In particular §4.4 distinguishes aggregate benchmark data from IRT item responses and notes that benchmark partitioning can change effective weight.

Exact, transferable descriptions of the numerical evidence:

- §3.1.1 and Fig. 3, pp. 5–6, predict METR task time horizons with roughly 60% training and 40% validation data. The plotted training R² is.852, **test R².753**, and test RMSE 16.623 minutes. The fitted mapping is exp(3.69C − 4.58). The caption/body’s rounded R².85 should not be described as held-out R². The dataset has roughly 26 overlapping models; benchmark comparisons with few overlaps are unstable. Twenty of 38 benchmarks have fewer than five overlaps and are excluded from that comparison; among 18 remaining benchmarks, median R² is.62, while some stronger individual associations rely on at most 13 points.
- §3.1.2 reports mean actual-versus-predicted benchmark R².71 for benchmarks with more than 25 scores and.81 for those with more than 40. These descriptions do not establish an independently held-out test for those particular averages. Appendix E.4 separately reports 10-fold cross-validation and alternative-link results in Table 9: sigmoid R².8641, MSE.0098; clipped linear R².8683, MSE.0095; both 253 parameters. The table title combines fit and cross-validation results, so avoid treating every displayed criterion as an independently held-out statistic without checking code.
- §3.2.1 estimates post-2023 frontier progress at.55 capability units/year, 95% interval.45–.67. Appendix E.5’s longer-period analysis gives about.42, interval.37–.46. This change with the time window is material. Forecasts extrapolate the fitted frontier and are not observed results. The main text’s approximately five-month task-horizon doubling conflicts with the footnote’s approximately four months; Fig. 5’s 1.8-unit three-year change also differs from the main text’s 1.65-unit calculation. Do not propagate these into an EEE headline.
- §3.2.2 estimates within-family compute coefficient k averaging.168 across three Llama families, each with four models; frontier-quality b changes.297/year. The central implied compute reduction is about 6×/year, with broad 1–50× uncertainty across family estimates. These are conditional estimates using external training-compute inputs, not causal estimates from a randomized design.
- §3.3’s acceleration detector is tested on synthetic benchmark/model data. Its false-positive rate averages 38%, ranging 14–54% depending on noise and window; the paper therefore presents it as a flag for investigation. Historical fitted slopes are.352 and.689 capability units/year, ratio 1.95, below the detector’s 2× threshold. Fig. 9 marks 2024-03-23 whereas the text calls the breakpoint April 2024.
- Appendix D simulates 600 models and 30 benchmarks with nonuniform score retention, keeping performance between 5% and 95%. This excludes floor/ceiling cases relevant to emerging difficult benchmarks. Some main-text and appendix scenario counts/acceleration factors differ; exact reimplementation should resolve these rather than blend them. A normality KS p-value.81 coexists with rejection by other residual tests; it is not proof of normal errors.
- Appendix E.3.2 estimates optimized-for versus other benchmark capability difference.12 units; permutation p-values range.10–.37, mean.22. “Optimized-for” means a frontier lab publicly reported a benchmark, not demonstrated training contamination or actual optimization. The description also says 9 + 10 benchmarks, then refers to 20 anchors and 40 fits; flag that count inconsistency before exact reproduction.

**Visually inspected figures:** Figs. 1–2, p. 4; Figs. 3–4, p. 6; Fig. 5, p. 8; Fig. 6, p. 9; Fig. 7, p. 10; Fig. 8, p. 12; Fig. 9, p. 13; Figs. 15–17, pp. 29–30, 32; Fig. 20, p. 35. Figs. 1–2 show capability/difficulty against release date and rankings; difficulty bars for sparse or near-floor benchmarks are enormous compared with model bars. Those bars are perturbations needed to increase fit loss by 5%, **not sampling confidence intervals**. Fig. 3 uses capability versus time horizon and predicted versus actual time horizons on logarithmic minute axes, with substantial scatter and the printed train/test statistics above. Fig. 4 plots predicted against actual [0,1] performance: Claude is systematically above the diagonal on SWE-Bench and below it on GeoBench, demonstrating family-by-domain residuals that a common C misses. Fig. 5 is a widening forecast band, not retrospective evidence of constant growth. Fig. 6 shows later frontier releases above a trend fitted before September 2024. Fig. 7 plots capability versus log training FLOP, with slope.215 and R².787 printed on the figure; this is a different pooled fit from the within-family k estimate. Fig. 8 illustrates synthetic detection at.26 years; Fig. 9 shows the historical fitted breakpoint and slopes. Fig. 15 compares coverage filters of at least one versus at least three benchmarks, similar central trends with noticeable individual outliers. Fig. 16’s bars are variation across choice of anchor, labeled mean ± SD; they are not the 5%-loss bars or a sampling CI. Fig. 17 has a capability-difference histogram centered.1205 and scatter against equality with some large outliers. Fig. 20’s 38 predicted/actual panels show both broad agreement on well-covered benchmarks and broad scatter (e.g. GeoBench) or narrow low-score coverage (e.g. Cybench, FrontierMath Tier 4, GSO-Bench). A strong overall fit does not erase these heterogeneous failures.

**DLCI consequence:** benchmark-level common-scale estimation is established prior art. Scope any contribution around provenance, declared heterogeneous outcome units, frozen setup identities, redundancy-aware domain weighting, transparent direct versus inferred components, and prospectively validated drift/failure gates. Location/scale normalization, low residual error, correlation and construct invariance are separate claims. Family residuals and floor/ceiling coverage must directly affect whether a temporal comparison is released.

## Gate design implications, with boundaries

| Gate question | Evidence and proposed test | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Are respondents and observations stable? | Immutable model weights/endpoint plus prompt, scaffold, budget, question release and grader identity; rerun or rescore shared artifacts when scoring changes. SL01 pp. 25–26, 41–44; SL04 A.5; SL05 B. | A repeated model name is not sufficient. |
| Is overlap informative in the claimed range? | Independent bridge count, family/source diversity, range coverage, residual/uncertainty profiles, floor and ceiling diagnostics; hold out observed bridge scores. SL01 p. 49; SL03 Fig. 6; SL05 Figs. 1,15,20. | No source validates an EEE minimum bridge count, and item-anchor counts cannot supply one. |
| Does the fit transport across groups? | Unrestricted subgroup fits and held-out family/time/setup prediction, compare offsets and slopes as well as ranks. SL02; SL03 Figs. 4–5; SL05 Fig. 4. | Pooled R² or fixed slopes do not establish measurement invariance or absence of DIF. Aggregate subgroup residual tests are analogues, not item-level DIF analyses. |
| Does content remain the same construct? | Track domain/task composition and weights; compare domain profiles and alternative latent dimensions; investigate stable ranks with level drift. SL01 pp. 35–37; SL04 §3.4; SL05 §4.3–4.4. | Numerical parameter freezing is not construct freezing. Correlation is not an invariance test. |
| Are chains accumulating error? | Direct late-to-early links, alternative paths/cycles and withheld bridge comparisons; propagate parameter dependence instead of treating links as independent by default. SL01 Figs. 10–11. | A connected graph or a consistent single chain is not independent validation. |
| Is uncertainty interpretable? | Separate sampling/run, estimation, anchor-choice, fit-form and path sensitivities; label each precisely. SL01 p. 49; SL03 chain CIs; SL05 Figs. 1,16. | A 5%-loss sensitivity bar or cross-anchor SD is not a calibrated CI. |

## Review limits

All five included texts were read fully; all figure claims name actual rendered pages inspected. Approximate visual readings are explicitly separated from exact printed numbers. No new data were collected, no EEE score was calibrated, no DLCI gate has passed, and no reviewed study establishes EEE-specific equivalence. The selected sources are strong for distinguishing designs and identifying failure modes, but they do not exhaust longitudinal IRT, formal DIF or construct-drift theory. The file deliberately records version, count and formula inconsistencies rather than repairing source evidence silently.
