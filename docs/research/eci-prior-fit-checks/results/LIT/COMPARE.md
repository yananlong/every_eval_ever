# Predicting benchmark scores from sparse matrices: BenchPress and PKPS compared

Two 2026 preprints predict unmeasured benchmark scores from a sparse model-by-benchmark record. Zeng and Papailiopoulos (arXiv 2606.24020v2, "You Don't Need to Run Every Eval") complete a matrix of publicly reported aggregate scores with a method they call BenchPress. Helm, Ghosh, Johnson and Priebe (arXiv 2611.03614, "Benchmark Prediction with Sparse and Unpaired Cached Responses") embed models from their item-level responses in a Product Kernel Perspective Space (PKPS) and regress scores on the embedding. This report compares the two on model, data, protocol and evidence, using the memory packs in `helm2611/` and `zeng2606/` and the audit in `results-audit.json`. The packs carry a line pointer for every fact, and a verdict name in parentheses is the audit's bounded verdict for the claim just stated.

Both papers are also open ICLR 2027 submissions, each with three official reviews and author responses on OpenReview (forum 7ChRYhe6kG for PKPS, forum TO4Jk7wG05 for BenchPress). Neither thread holds a meta-review or a decision at retrieval on 2026-11-25. Statements taken from the threads are cited by OpenReview note id, the identifier of each post, and "the notes" below means those posts. They are reviewer or author statements made in a rebuttal discussion and are not audited, and the audit verdict names in this report apply only to the claims the audit covered. Some author statements describe revisions or analyses that may not be in the arXiv versions the packs summarise, and we have not reproduced the new results the authors report.

One correction to how the Helm paper is often summarised comes first. Its contribution is PKPS. Low-rank matrix completion (LRMC) enters as the baseline, implemented as the BenchPress recipe, and as one of two components of the completion ensemble. The Every Eval Ever (EEE) datastore supplies the second of two test suites, as a dense 45 by 16 block. <!-- X-PROB-CORRECTION -->

| | BenchPress (Zeng and Papailiopoulos) | PKPS (Helm et al.) |
|---|---|---|
| Target | publicly reported aggregate score | true full-task mean score |
| Input | sparse matrix of aggregate scores | cached query and response text per item |
| Core model | additive offsets plus rank-2 interaction, ALS, ridge 0.1 | product-kernel affinity, classical MDS (d = 8), K-NN (K = 5) |
| Transform | logit, then per-benchmark standardisation | none for query efficiency; BenchPress pipeline for completion |
| Dimension chosen by | held-out error of Soft-Impute | fixed a priori |
| Data | 84 by 133, 2,604 cells, 23.3% filled | HELM-Lite 93 by 18 and EEE 45 by 16, dense, then masked |
| Missingness in evaluation | random holdout of observed cells | uniform masking |
| Holdout unit | cell (per model), plus a temporal test | cell, with the target developer's models excluded from regression |
| Uncertainty | risk score and conformal intervals | SEM over seeds only |

## The problem each solves

BenchPress asks whether any subset of a model's reported scores predicts the rest, with neither the inputs nor the targets fixed in advance. Its target is the number a public source would report. In the authors' words, the method predicts "what this public matrix would extrapolate to rather than what a controlled re-evaluation under a single configuration would yield". <!-- X-PROB-Z -->

PKPS targets the score a model would obtain on a full task, given cached responses that cover few tasks and share few queries with other models. It treats two tasks as one problem indexed by the number of observed queries in the target cell. With some queries observed, the task is query-efficient evaluation. With none, it is completion, and the model is placed in the embedding by its responses to other tasks. <!-- X-PROB-H -->

The two papers therefore differ in estimand before they differ in method, since a BenchPress prediction inherits whatever configuration the source used, while a PKPS prediction is tied to one scoring function on one query set. On query efficiency, PKPS at one query per cell reaches an error of 0.070 on the EEE suite, below the 0.081 that the cell's own sample mean reaches at eight queries, which is the basis of the paper's "up to 8x" statement. On the HELM suite the corresponding factor is 4, and the sample mean overtakes PKPS as the budget grows (audit verdict supports_exploratory_follow_up). <!-- CLM-HELM-QE --> Those factors are measured against the cell's own sample mean. In the rebuttal the authors add a task-mean baseline (the average score of the other models on the task) and replace their item response baseline with a regularised Rasch model fitted on all sparse observations. On the EEE suite PKPS stays ahead of the Rasch baseline up to eight queries (0.070, 0.065 and 0.060 against 0.113, 0.102 and 0.069 at one, two and eight queries) and is well below the EEE task mean of 0.117. On HELM the authors describe the two as similar at the smallest budgets and the Rasch model as better at larger ones (0.071 against 0.108 at eight queries), which suggests that the HELM gain over the sample mean largely disappears against a score-only baseline that uses the other models (notes EZwq1LrjP3 and MQXm9aHPoq). <!-- R-H-EZwq1LrjP3 --> <!-- R-H-MQXm9aHPoq -->

## Statistical model and identifiability

BenchPress works in standardised logit space. The prediction for model m on benchmark b is a global mean, a model offset, a benchmark offset and a rank-2 interaction (U V')_mb. The offsets are the observed means, computed before the interaction is fit. U and V minimise squared error on observed cells plus a ridge penalty with weight 0.1, by alternating least squares averaged over 10 initialisations. The paper, as the pack records it, states no probabilistic model, but the objective is the penalised Gaussian log-likelihood of an implicit one. The squared-error term is the negative log-likelihood of independent Gaussian noise with a common variance in standardised logit space, and the ridge term is a Gaussian prior on the rows of U and V, with the weight 0.1 standing for a fixed ratio of noise variance to prior variance, so alternating least squares finds a local maximum a posteriori estimate. Neither variance is estimated or used again. The offsets are plug-in means computed before the fit and are not part of the same optimisation, the average over initialisations is an ensemble over local optima and not a posterior mean, and the column standardisation fixes each benchmark's noise scale from the cohort's spread, so the implicit noise model is not a sampling model for a bounded score and does not depend on item count or source. Prediction intervals come from a separate conformal calibration (see the section on uncertainty) and not from this model. <!-- X-MODEL-Z -->

Two identifiability points follow from that objective, and they are our reading, since the paper does not discuss them. A model row is described by three numbers (an offset and two factor scores), and the fitted matrix can have rank 4 in transformed space. The abstract's statement that a model's scores are "largely determined by just two numbers" matches the Soft-Impute parameterisation used for rank selection, which has no offsets. The factor pair is also determined only up to an invertible 2 by 2 transformation, and the paper's reading of a general-capability axis and a domain-lean axis comes from an SVD of the completed matrix, which fixes that freedom by convention. <!-- X-IDENT-Z -->

PKPS has no parametric score model. For each pair of models it computes an affinity, a query-kernel-weighted average of response-kernel similarities over all pairs of queries the two models answered. That affinity is converted to a squared dissimilarity and embedded by classical multidimensional scaling in 8 dimensions, and a distance-weighted nearest-neighbour regressor then maps the embedding to scores. The query kernel is an RBF whose bandwidth is cross-validated, and the response kernel is linear on PCA-reduced, unit-normalised embeddings from `gemini-embedding-001`. Exact query matching recovers the authors' earlier paired method, DKPS, as a special case. <!-- X-MODEL-H -->

The rebuttal adds details on the query kernel. A constant query kernel, the limit of infinite bandwidth, is also a special case of PKPS, and under the paper's linear response kernel it comes close to the cross-validated bandwidth at small budgets (0.064 against 0.065 on EEE and 0.125 against 0.125 on HELM at two queries, constant kernel first), while the authors find exact matching best at 32 queries (notes MQXm9aHPoq and aSnt2mSphk). <!-- R-H-MQXm9aHPoq --> <!-- R-H-aSnt2mSphk --> Reviewer VZXd's reply holds that the benefit of query similarity remains unclear under the linear kernel (note OYVPBOIBuW). <!-- R-H-OYVPBOIBuW --> The authors also add a correctness response kernel, which represents each response by its item score alone, and report that intermediate bandwidths beat both the constant kernel and exact matching at every budget under it, so that how much query similarity helps depends on the response kernel (notes MQXm9aHPoq and aSnt2mSphk). Their table puts the margin over the constant kernel at up to 0.021 on HELM (0.059 against 0.080 at 32 queries) and at most 0.002 on EEE. Bandwidth selection uses the leave-one-model-out error of the K-NN regressor on the observed sample scores of all models, with K = 8 in the tuning step and K = 5 in the final regressor. The authors call that mismatch unintentional and report that using K = 5 in both changes MAE by 0.001 on average and at most 0.003 (note CjPsv3uqNp). <!-- R-H-CjPsv3uqNp -->

A rigid motion of the representation leaves nearest-neighbour regression unchanged, so the embedding is identified as far as prediction needs. Theorem 1 shows that it concentrates around a population counterpart when kernels are bounded, the cross-model query-kernel mass is positive and an eigengap exists, with a bound that grows linearly in the number of models and is not evaluated on either suite. <!-- X-IDENT-H --> Theorem 2 states that a bandwidth-selected PKPS estimator is no worse than DKPS and is strictly better when query sets are disjoint. The estimator in the theorem uses a three-way sample split, an out-of-sample extension and a guarded selection rule, and the experiments use none of these. The theorem therefore motivates the experimental method without covering it (audit verdict internally_consistent_only). <!-- CLM-HELM-THEORY -->

The rebuttal adds checks and concessions on how far the theory reaches. The authors report a cross-model query-kernel mass of 0.58 on HELM and 0.88 on EEE and a clear gap after the eighth eigenvalue on both suites. They describe score regularity as only approximately true in practice and model-support denseness as likely untrue, though standard in the analysis of nearest-neighbour methods (notes kfHInn7ikf and flQGeCyN5F). <!-- R-H-kfHInn7ikf --> <!-- R-H-flQGeCyN5F --> They state that Assumption 7 (pairing deficiency), which the strict improvement over DKPS in Theorem 2 requires, is not true of the curated HELM and EEE data and that the experiments create it by subsampling responses (note flQGeCyN5F). <!-- R-H-flQGeCyN5F --> After that subsampling, models share 2.7% to 11% of their queries on HELM and 1.1% to 4.4% on EEE (note kfHInn7ikf). <!-- R-H-kfHInn7ikf --> On the estimator, the authors say the guarded selector was a theoretical device absent from the original implementation, and that re-running with it at 95% confidence changes MAE by 0.001 on average and at most 0.004 across both suites and 1, 2, 8 and 32 queries (notes kfHInn7ikf and CjPsv3uqNp). <!-- R-H-kfHInn7ikf --> <!-- R-H-CjPsv3uqNp --> That narrows one of the three gaps described above, the selection rule, and the notes report no comparable check for the three-way sample split or the out-of-sample extension.

## Item versus benchmark granularity

BenchPress uses one number per model and benchmark. PKPS needs the text of each query and each response, and it cannot run on a leaderboard that publishes only scores. <!-- X-GRAN --> This limits where each applies. In the Helm authors' Space build of 2026-11-24, 148 of 10,520 model identities in the EEE store had response coordinates. The Helm paper itself notes that most large public leaderboards include only scores. <!-- X-GRAN-COVER --> The PKPS authors repeat the constraint in the rebuttal, writing that PKPS needs item-level responses and that most large public leaderboards release only aggregate scores (note A1AaAgtk2N). <!-- R-H-A1AaAgtk2N --> They also add a correctness response kernel that represents a response by its item score, so that variant does not need response text, though it still needs a score for each observed query (note MQXm9aHPoq). This qualifies the statement above that PKPS needs the text of each response. <!-- R-H-MQXm9aHPoq -->

Granularity also changes what "sparse" means. A BenchPress cell is either observed or missing, whereas a PKPS cell can be partly observed, and the paper's query-efficiency results concern that case.

The BenchPress rebuttal reports an item-level extension. Reviewer ye3d asks whether the method extends to questions instead of benchmarks (note i0gHj2lV81), and the authors answer with a run on the Open LLM Leaderboard v2, a matrix of 425 models and 21,176 questions over 37 subtasks (note dDTi0kQAg5). <!-- R-Z-i0gHj2lV81 --> The rank is raised from 2 to 40 by rounding the subtask count up to the nearest multiple of ten, which the authors call a simple heuristic. The run compares BenchPress with a ridge predictor and an item response model on binary answer accuracy. BenchPress has the highest mean accuracy at 200 and 400 questions, and the ridge predictor is more accurate at larger budgets. The comparison includes no PKPS, so the notes give no head-to-head between the two methods at item level. <!-- R-Z-dDTi0kQAg5 -->

## The transform

BenchPress compares seven transforms across twelve methods. For its adopted method, the logit gives a held-out median absolute error (MedAE) of 4.63 points against 5.17 for raw scores. Probit gives 4.64 and the quantile transform 4.73, so the gain comes from unbounding the scale more than from the logistic form. Three benchmarks on rating scales are left untransformed. <!-- X-TRANS-Z -->

Helm et al. do not study the transform. For completion they reuse the BenchPress pipeline, and PKPS regresses the same standardised logit residuals that LRMC factorises, so the two differ only in how the residual is modelled. For query efficiency the regression targets are raw full-task scores. <!-- X-TRANS-H -->

Neither paper models sampling noise in the scores. The BenchPress matrix contains benchmarks with 6 to 30 items, and its logit clip constant is not stated. <!-- X-TRANS-NOISE -->

## How rank is chosen

BenchPress gives two lines of evidence for rank 2. Its first is a sweep of Soft-Impute over hard rank, in which held-out error is lowest at rank 2 in both raw and logit space. When the appendix repeats the sweep with the newest 17 models held out as a block, the logit-space minimum stays at rank 2, while in raw space ranks 2 and 3 fall within each other's bootstrap interval (audit verdict supports_exploratory_follow_up). <!-- CLM-ZENG-RANK-HELDOUT -->

That sweep covers one estimator. Under the same nested validation, non-negative matrix factorisation selects rank 1 for all seven transforms and probabilistic matrix factorisation selects rank 3 or 5, and the rank of the adopted Bias ALS is fixed at 2 and never swept. The selected rank therefore depends on the estimator and on the sparsity level as well as on the data. <!-- X-RANK-METHOD -->

The second line is that two components explain 91.2% to 97.6% of the variance in four fully observed submatrices. Those blocks measure 4 by 42, 7 by 11, 10 by 7 and 13 by 6 (benchmarks by models), so the 4 by 42 block has at most 4 components and the 13 by 6 block, once centred, at most 5. Within them the first component alone explains 78.2% to 90.4% and the stable rank is 1.11 to 1.28, so the table supports one dominant factor at least as well as it supports two (audit verdict internally_consistent_only). <!-- CLM-ZENG-RANK-VARIANCE -->

Helm et al. fix the embedding dimension at 8, following their earlier paper. Their sensitivity analysis finds dimensions 4 and 8 within 0.003 in error and dimension 24 up to 0.018 worse. They inherit rank 2 and ridge 0.1 for LRMC without re-selecting them for suites of 16 or 18 tasks and cohorts as small as 10 models. <!-- X-RANK-H --> In the rebuttal's embedding-model comparison on EEE, the authors instead select the response PCA dimension and the MDS dimension by the same cross-validation as the bandwidth, for both PKPS and DKPS (note A1AaAgtk2N), so the fixed dimension of 8 describes the paper's main runs and not every run reported in the thread. <!-- R-H-A1AaAgtk2N -->

An exploratory in-house rerun (`runs/BENCHPRESS/rank_repro_contrasts.csv`, one hide-half split, 95% model-bootstrap intervals) supplies the sweep the paper omits. On the May matrix, Bias ALS improves from rank 1 to rank 2 by 0.53 MedAE points [0.27, 0.70], and rank 3 changes it by a further 0.05 with an interval of [-0.27, 0.13] that spans zero. Hard-rank imputation without offsets gains 1.38 points [0.95, 1.87] from rank 1 to rank 2 and loses 0.48 at rank 3. The rerun's absolute errors are higher than the paper's (6.41 for rank-2 Bias ALS), so it reproduces the ordering of ranks and does not reproduce the headline figure, and it does not collapse version families, which may account for part of the rank-2 gain. <!-- X-RANK-REPRO -->

The Space offers one more data point, which is build output whose rank and ridge were tuned on the cross-validation it reports. On the whole EEE store its model is a single ability per model with a per-metric intercept and slope, followed by a rank-8 residual. Cross-validated error is 13.85 points for the metric mean, 10.77 for the one-ability model and 10.38 with the residual added. <!-- X-RANK-SPACE -->

The BenchPress review thread presses on the rank-2 reading. Reviewer sCbk notes that ranks 3 to 6 give similar errors, so that choosing rank 2 for simplicity does not show that models have exactly two capability dimensions. The same reviewer observes that the 59 by 25 benchmark-selection check mainly compared selected sets with random ones, and asks for model-mean and offsets-only baselines there (note 5IjLz8yPYM). <!-- R-Z-5IjLz8yPYM --> The authors agree that the exact number of dimensions is not established and that the rank and the factor interpretation may change as models and benchmarks are added. They defend rank 2 with the finding that two components explain over 90% of the variance in the four fully observed submatrices, and with the second component of the completed matrix, which contrasts long-context and coding benchmarks with agentic-search and competition-math benchmarks (note o5UgbYmeyg). <!-- R-Z-o5UgbYmeyg --> That reply does not address the block sizes or the first-component shares discussed above. On the added split of 59 reference models and 25 held-out models, the authors report that model mean clearly underperforms BenchPress and offsets only, that offsets alone perform well, and that BenchPress has the lower MedAE at most budgets, with a gap to offsets less consistent than in the main experiment, possibly because fewer training models limit the rank-two correction (note 5VA0iC7OhF). The notes link that comparison as a plot and do not tabulate it. <!-- R-Z-5VA0iC7OhF --> This agrees in direction with the flat step from rank 2 to rank 3 for Bias ALS in the rerun above, and it fits the observation in the agreement section below that offsets carry most of the predictable structure.

## Missingness assumptions

BenchPress states that its matrix has structured missingness, with popular models and popular benchmarks over-represented, and that this violates the uniform-sampling assumption behind matrix-completion guarantees. It fits no missingness model. Its held-out cells are random draws from observed cells, so its error figures describe cells that someone chose to run and report. The 76.7% of cells never observed are not represented. Most scores come from the model provider's own materials, under the configuration the vendor foregrounds, which makes the observed matrix closer to best-reported than to typical performance. <!-- X-MISS-Z -->

Helm et al. start from dense matrices and remove cells and queries uniformly at random. Their theory assumes uniform subsampling. They name the gap themselves, writing that missingness in deployed evaluation data "correlates with developer, release date, and expected performance", and they leave it to future work. <!-- X-MISS-H -->

As published, both evaluations therefore measure error under missingness that is ignorable by construction or by convention. The Space README says directly that store scores are not missing at random and that its folds inherit that selection. Its own stress test hides the strongest fifth of each collection and finds a bias of about -9.4 points, which indicates how far selection on performance can move the answer. <!-- X-MISS-SPACE -->

The PKPS rebuttal adds simulated structure to its dense suites. In reply to a reviewer, the authors hold out every subtask of one parent benchmark for each target model, and separately let each model draw its queries with a bias toward hard or easy items, the direction differing across models (note CjPsv3uqNp). With a whole benchmark held out and 50% of cells observed, LRMC error on HELM is 0.133 against 0.106 under uniform masking, and adding PKPS lowers it to 0.124 against 0.099, which the authors read as a gain about as large as in the uniform case. Under difficulty-biased coverage at two queries, the LRMC plus sample baseline rises from 0.092 to 0.184 on HELM and from 0.062 to 0.128 on EEE. The authors report PKPS alone moving from 0.125 to 0.132 on HELM and from 0.065 to 0.090 on EEE, and PKPS added to the baseline giving 0.131 and 0.091. <!-- R-H-CjPsv3uqNp --> This is the closest either thread comes to selection on outcomes, though it biases which queries a model is observed on and does not model a developer's choice of what to publish. Reviewer dSNB's reply still objects to the reliance on artificially simulated sparsity over naturally incomplete data (note 86iBE4plOr). <!-- R-H-86iBE4plOr --> The authors answer that scoring a method requires a full-evaluation score, so the sparsity has to be induced, and that arena-style evaluations are natively unpaired and sparse (note uxGr4GA2Ct). They cite 45% shared queries between two models on average across the 7 arena-style benchmarks in the EEE datastore (note flQGeCyN5F). <!-- R-H-uxGr4GA2Ct --> <!-- R-H-flQGeCyN5F --> Neither thread reports a test of missingness driven by selective publication. The PKPS additions mask or bias queries on dense data, and the BenchPress thread concerns later models and newly reported scores.

## Held-out protocol and leakage risk

BenchPress hides a random share of each model's observed scores over 10 seeds and 3 folds, tunes on nested validation cells, and reports medians. It adds a block holdout of the newest models and a temporal test that trains only on models released before the target. <!-- X-PROT-Z --> Its headline of 4.63 points is a median, with the benchmark mean at 10.04 and the model mean at 7.22 under the same protocol (audit verdict supports_exploratory_follow_up). <!-- CLM-ZENG-HELDOUT-ERROR -->

Near-duplicate rows and columns are the main leakage risk in a public score matrix, and BenchPress handles them in two ways. It keeps one canonical setting per model and one column per benchmark family, and it tests what happens when strong peers or same-provider models are masked. Masking strongly correlated peers raises error, while masking same-provider models gives p = 0.011 and 0.094 on its two metrics, which the paper does not treat as a rejection at its 0.01 threshold. <!-- X-LEAK -->

Two of its headline numbers are affected by how cells are counted. The five-probe result of 3.93 points selects the probe set by minimising error on the same cells that are then reported, and counts the probe cells themselves as zero error. The body says the curves "should not be read as held-out transfer estimates", and the abstract drops that caveat (audit verdict does_not_support_claim). <!-- CLM-ZENG-PROBE --> The temporal test also counts revealed cells as zero error, so the fall in error as more seed scores are revealed is partly mechanical. [EVIDENCE NEEDED: temporal error computed on hidden cells only, with a model-mean baseline at each number of seed scores] <!-- CLM-ZENG-TEMPORAL -->

The review thread bears on that marker without meeting it, and what it reports is unaudited. Reviewer sCbk observes that the temporal cutoff uses release dates while the date on which each score was reported is not recorded, so a training matrix can hold scores for older models that were reported after the target's release (note 5IjLz8yPYM). <!-- R-Z-5IjLz8yPYM --> The authors add that limitation to the text and run a frozen test. It fixes the April 30 matrix, predicts the 23 models added in the August 26 snapshot from April benchmarks only, and scores each budget on test scores outside the revealed set. Its MedAE is 7.98, 6.35 and 6.15 at 1, 5 and 10 revealed scores, against 9.37, 6.76 and 6.12 for the 27 targets of the main-body temporal test (note 0AU8mOG0kG). <!-- R-Z-0AU8mOG0kG --> The frozen test therefore reports error on hidden cells, but it has no model-mean baseline and uses different targets, and the notes do not say whether the main-body column counts revealed cells as zero error.

The same thread reports that the model mean can beat BenchPress on newer models. Reviewer 5ChR read the original Table 21 as showing that BenchPress is not guaranteed to beat the model mean in sparse public score settings (note ZDMaKlrU9e). <!-- R-Z-ZDMaKlrU9e --> The authors agree for one or two known scores on the newer, stronger models added later, which they attribute to distribution shift from the older, weaker models in the main matrix, since BenchPress is fit mostly on those older models while the model mean is fit directly on each new model's known scores. They replaced the table, whose rows had been scored on different test cells, with one that uses the same test cells at each number of revealed scores. Under cost-unaware probes, MedAE for BenchPress against the model mean is 9.13 against 7.52 with one revealed score, 7.27 against 6.93 with two and 6.47 against 7.21 with three. Under cost-aware probes it is 6.52 against 6.09, 6.63 against 6.20 and 5.70 against 7.08. These rest on 11 and 8 models, a sample the authors call small (note qFhIqdhOhl). <!-- R-Z-qFhIqdhOhl --> They contrast this with the main experiment, a random holdout inside the original matrix, where BenchPress reaches 4.63 against 7.22 for the model mean (note 5VA0iC7OhF). <!-- R-Z-5VA0iC7OhF --> Reviewer sCbk's own comparison for later-snapshot models gives 6.88 for BenchPress and 6.95 for the model mean with five unrestricted probes, without a clear bootstrap improvement (note 5IjLz8yPYM). <!-- R-Z-5IjLz8yPYM --> The cost-aware probes come from a 25-benchmark low-cost allowlist. The authors build it from a rough automated estimate of the dollar cost of one full evaluation run, taken from public records such as Artificial Analysis, MathArena or a benchmark's own paper or leaderboard, and then manually correct estimates they judge misleading (note qFhIqdhOhl). <!-- R-Z-qFhIqdhOhl -->

Helm et al. average over 16 seeds of random masking. Their regressor is fit leave-one-family-out, meaning that every model from the target's developer is excluded from its training set. This guard against sibling leakage is stricter than anything in BenchPress. The embedding is transductive, meaning that the target model is embedded jointly with the reference models. No scores enter the embedding, so labels cannot leak through it, though the target remains in sample for the representation. <!-- X-PROT-H --> In the rebuttal the added LRMC baseline is fitted under the same supervision and leave-one-family-out validation as PKPS (note CjPsv3uqNp). <!-- R-H-CjPsv3uqNp -->

On completion, the ensemble of PKPS and LRMC lowers error from 0.096 to 0.069 on the EEE suite at full cohort, the 28% in the abstract. On HELM at full cohort the change is 0.105 to 0.100 and is not significant. The margin is largest where rank-2 LRMC is least identified, and LRMC's rank was not re-tuned for these small matrices (audit verdict supports_exploratory_follow_up). <!-- CLM-HELM-COMPLETION -->

## Uncertainty quantification

BenchPress attaches a reliability layer to its point predictions. A small model trained on other folds predicts each cell's absolute error from predictor disagreement and from matrix-support features such as observation counts and the strongest peer correlation. The resulting risk score orders predictions usefully, with a MedAE of 1.83 among the most trusted 20% of cells. <!-- CLM-ZENG-RELIABILITY --> The same score scales split-conformal intervals. The 90% interval is 27.01 points wide and the 80% interval 19.56, and empirical coverage is reported only for an 80% variant on 23 benchmarks. <!-- X-UQ-Z -->

Set beside the abstract, these widths change how "within 4.6 points" should be read, because the figure is a median and the paper's own 90% interval for the same predictor is about plus or minus 13.5 points. <!-- CLM-ZENG-HELDOUT-ERROR -->

The review thread cites a coverage figure that the pack for the preprint does not carry. Reviewer sCbk reports that the 90% prediction intervals cover 89.9% of held-out scores (note 5IjLz8yPYM), and the authors' table gives 89.9% coverage with a median width of 28.0 points over 26,040 predictions in the original held-out evaluation (note o5UgbYmeyg). <!-- R-Z-5IjLz8yPYM --> <!-- R-Z-o5UgbYmeyg --> That qualifies the statement above that coverage is reported only for an 80% variant on 23 benchmarks, and the 28.0 median width is close to the 27.01 points quoted above. The notes do not say whether the two widths summarise the same statistic or whether the submission under review differs from the preprint, so the earlier statement is qualified here and not withdrawn. Under shift the authors report lower coverage. With the reference matrix, reliability estimator and calibration frozen at the April 30 matrix, the nominal 90% intervals cover 84.3% of 178 newly reported scores for existing models (median width 22.4) and 86.8% of 4,540 predictions for 23 models added by the August 26 snapshot (median width 27.7) (note o5UgbYmeyg). <!-- R-Z-o5UgbYmeyg --> The authors restrict the near-nominal coverage claim to the original held-out setting and recommend collecting recent scores, updating the reference matrix and the reliability estimator, and recalibrating on separate recent held-out scores (note 0AU8mOG0kG). <!-- R-Z-0AU8mOG0kG --> Reviewer sCbk adds that predictions were too high for 65% of the 178 newly reported scores, which the reviewer does not read as a sign that the intervals are wrong but as a reason to check the reliability model on later data (note 5IjLz8yPYM). <!-- R-Z-5IjLz8yPYM -->

Helm et al. report standard errors over seeds and Wilcoxon tests over tasks, and no predictive uncertainty for a cell. Their Space adds a marginal interval, the 0.9 quantile of absolute held-out error within a collection, with empirical coverage of 0.899. It is not conditional on the model. <!-- X-UQ-H -->

## Data provenance and dependence on EEE and aggregators

The BenchPress matrix is assembled by crawling release blogs, system cards, technical reports and model cards, recursing through every competitor mentioned, and then sweeping six leaderboards. A priority rule resolves conflicts in favour of the vendor's release materials. Each value carries a URL. The matrix is a living artefact, and the paper and the dataset card disagree on which version is the "paper matrix". The experiments use the May 84 by 133 matrix, while the card gives 129 by 253 from the August update. <!-- X-PROV-Z -->

Helm et al. use two curated item-level sources. One is HELM-Lite, restricted to 93 models that ran all 18 tasks. The other is five EEE benchmarks, restricted to 45 models that answered every item. At the time, the EEE store held 36 benchmarks and 165 models with 15% of model and benchmark pairs evaluated. <!-- X-PROV-H -->

The rebuttal repeats those EEE figures as the reason that no larger fully evaluated block exists (note A1AaAgtk2N). <!-- R-H-A1AaAgtk2N --> It also adds a third collection beyond the two above, the Open LLM Leaderboard v1, with 367 models from 150 families and 16 tasks, a pool of 100 queries per task and sparsity induced by subsampling each model's queries. The authors describe its tasks as largely multiple choice or true and false. With the correctness response kernel they report PKPS as the most accurate method at one and two queries (0.079 and 0.061, against 0.102 and 0.085 for LRMC plus sample) and level with DKPS at eight (0.044 against 0.045) (note A1AaAgtk2N). <!-- R-H-A1AaAgtk2N --> The BenchPress rebuttal uses the v2 leaderboard (note dDTi0kQAg5), so the two extensions do not share a collection. <!-- R-Z-dDTi0kQAg5 -->

An in-house check of the released dataset (`runs/BENCHPRESS/bp_explore.json`, exploratory) settles the version question and adds one dependence. At dataset revision 1525e107 the May matrix is recoverable and reproduces the paper's 84 by 133 with 2,604 cells, while the current revision, a8db5a84 of 2026-08-18, holds 283 models, 712 benchmarks and 8,713 rows, of which 8,313 are accepted. EEE now carries BenchPress as a collection at that same revision, and 8,293 of the 8,313 accepted rows are in EEE with identical values. A matrix rebuilt from EEE today therefore contains the BenchPress matrix, and whether the EEE-derived matrix in the BenchPress appendix predates that ingestion is not established here. <!-- X-PROV-BP -->

Each paper leans on the other's ecosystem. BenchPress reports a run on an EEE-derived matrix of 653 by 498, with a MedAE of 2.89 against 7.00 for the per-benchmark median. Helm et al. take BenchPress as their completion baseline and preprocessing. <!-- X-PROV-CROSS -->

## What each would and would not detect

We argue this section from each method's inputs, since neither paper tests detection directly. BenchPress sees covariance between benchmarks across models, so it can detect that a benchmark is redundant with others. It also reports where prediction fails, namely when no close peer model or neighbour benchmark exists, when training models are old relative to the target, and for particular models, with per-model error varying about 40-fold. <!-- X-DET-Z-FAIL --> It cannot see anything inside a benchmark, and it cannot separate a capability difference from a difference in harness, prompt or reasoning budget, since both arrive as one number. Its ranking result, 92.1% of pairs ordered correctly at a five-point margin, has no baseline, so the increment over a trivial completion is unknown (audit verdict supports_exploratory_follow_up). <!-- CLM-ZENG-RANKING -->

PKPS sees how models answer. It can place a model that has a handful of responses and no shared queries, which a score matrix cannot. Its blind spots are models without cached responses, tasks where the embedding of a response says little about its correctness, and stochastic scoring, which the authors list as a limitation. <!-- X-DET -->

Neither method can detect selection on unobserved outcomes. A developer that reports only favourable benchmarks produces a matrix on which both methods validate well and extrapolate with bias.

## Agreement, disagreement and complementarity

The papers agree on the shape of the predictable structure. Offsets for model and task in logit space carry most of it, and a low-dimensional interaction adds a smaller increment. BenchPress's model mean gives 7.22 and its rank-2 interaction brings that to 4.63, while the Space's one-ability model gives 10.77 and its rank-8 residual brings that to 10.38. <!-- X-AGREE --> <!-- X-RANK-SPACE -->

They disagree on whether aggregate scores are enough. Helm et al. report that adding response-based representations lowers completion error by 5% to 28% relative to rank-2 LRMC under uniform masking. Their own Space, on the live store with a different embedder and cross-validation over model and collection pairs, shows LRMC at 0.0587 and the ensemble at 0.0577, a margin of 0.001. The protocols differ, so this is context for the paper's claim and does not refute it. <!-- X-DISAGREE -->

The PKPS rebuttal sharpens the disagreement in the query-efficiency setting. At reviewers' request the authors add LRMC in the BenchPress style, alone and blended with the cell's own sample mean, and call LRMC plus sample a strong baseline, especially on HELM (note CjPsv3uqNp). <!-- R-H-CjPsv3uqNp --> Adding PKPS to it moves HELM error from 0.107, 0.092, 0.065 and 0.033 to 0.106, 0.092, 0.064 and 0.033 at 1, 2, 8 and 32 observed queries. The authors report relative reductions of 1.2%, -0.1%, 1.6% and 0.0%, with Wilcoxon p of .28, .73, .02 and .44. On EEE the error moves from 0.073, 0.062, 0.049 and 0.031 to 0.065, 0.057, 0.046 and 0.030, relative reductions of 10.6%, 8.5%, 7.1% and 2.2%, with p below .001 for the first three budgets and .08 at 32 queries (note aSnt2mSphk). <!-- R-H-aSnt2mSphk --> Reviewer VZXd's reply calls the HELM improvement small, and the authors answer that it is small on HELM and non-trivial, upwards of 10%, on EEE (notes OYVPBOIBuW and aSnt2mSphk). <!-- R-H-OYVPBOIBuW --> The authors' statement that adding PKPS never hurts a score-only baseline holds to within 0.1%, since the HELM change at two queries is -0.1% (note CjPsv3uqNp). <!-- R-H-CjPsv3uqNp --> These are query-efficiency settings, so they are not directly comparable with the completion figures above, but they point the same way as the Space comparison, with the same caveat that the protocols differ. These figures suggest that on HELM most of what PKPS adds is already carried by a blend of aggregate scores and the cell's own sample, while on EEE at small budgets the response-based representation still adds a measurable margin.

The methods are complementary in inputs. Matrix completion covers every reported cell at no marginal cost. Response-based representations add most where a model has few scores, which is the regime of small cohorts and low coverage in which the Helm paper reports its largest gains. <!-- X-COMPLEMENT --> The PKPS authors describe their method in the same terms, as complementary to methods that use only score information (note MQXm9aHPoq), and report that pairing PKPS with score-only baselines helps most on EEE at small budgets (note CjPsv3uqNp). <!-- R-H-MQXm9aHPoq --> <!-- R-H-CjPsv3uqNp -->

## Relation to our ECI study

Epoch's ECI is a one-factor logistic item response model with Beta noise, fit to 2,879 benchmark-level scores from 256 models on 57 benchmarks. <!-- E-ECI-MODEL --> Its mean structure in logit space is a benchmark intercept plus a rank-1 interaction between benchmark slope and model capability. That places it between the BenchPress model-mean baseline and BenchPress itself, and it has the same form as the Space's one-ability stage.

Under Epoch's model, the registered verdict is that model adequacy (C2) is falsified. T1, T2 and T5 reject at a max-T adjusted p of 0.0053 against model-true replicates, and the localisation set holds 16 benchmarks. T1 measures item-curve shape. T2 is a local-dependence statistic built from the pairwise correlation of residuals over the 445 benchmark pairs that share at least 15 models. T5 is the largest developer-by-benchmark mean residual. The verdict record adds that T2 and T5 had no demonstrated power in the calibration stage, so they could reject but could not have supported adequacy. <!-- E-ECI-PPC -->

The exploratory model expansion (W6) did not produce a two-dimensional fit. Its two-dimensional capability candidate, F1, reached the time cap at both sampler settings and is recorded as not fitted. Three other W6 candidates converged. Developer-by-benchmark offsets (F2) gain 209.7 in PSIS-LOO elpd (SE 20.4), per-model noise (F5) gains 44.0 (SE 11.6), and time drift (F4) changes it by -0.6 (SE 2.5). A benchmark-family variant fitted alongside W6 (FX_lin) gains 242.4 (SE 20.1). <!-- E-ECI-W6 -->

W7 is a further exploratory stage outside every registered verdict, and it is still running. Of its five candidates on Epoch's data, the one that converged is G3, which combines developer-by-benchmark offsets with benchmark-family offsets. The candidates that add a second capability dimension with zero-sum loadings (G2, G4), an asymmetric item curve (G1) or all features (G5) are recorded as not fitted. G3 gains 379.4 in PSIS-LOO elpd over the base model (SE 25.8), with a partial K-fold gain of 113.8 (SE 15.2) on the 2 of 5 folds that finished. <!-- E-ECI-EXT -->

Whether G3 removes the misfit is pending. The W7 rule requires that no check reject at 0.01 against 120 model-true replicates simulated from and refitted with G3 itself. The stored summary covers 12 finished replicates, which cannot support a rejection at that level, and it records none. An approximate comparison against the base model's 187 replicates gives T2 an adjusted p of 0.0053 (z of 9.07) and T5 an adjusted p of 1.0. That approximation suggests residual correlation between benchmarks persists under G3 while the developer signal is absorbed, and it is not the registered test. For F2 alone the calibration is finished, and over 115 converged replicates T1 and T2 still reject while T5 does not. <!-- E-ECI-W7-PENDING -->

The two papers bear on this in four ways, all indirect. The first is that BenchPress's second factor separates domains, and residual correlation between benchmarks is what such a factor would leave behind in a one-factor fit, which suggests that the T2 rejection and the BenchPress rank result point at the same kind of departure without identifying its form. BenchPress finds that masking same-category benchmarks does not change error (p = 0.832 and 0.725), which indicates that its second factor is not well described by category labels, whereas G3 encodes structure through benchmark families. <!-- E-ECI-LINK -->

The second is direct evidence on Epoch's own matrix, from the same exploratory rerun cited above. Hard-rank imputation on the 256 by 57 matrix improves by 1.98 MedAE points [1.54, 2.39] from rank 1 to rank 2 and worsens by 0.43 at rank 3. With offsets, rank 2 improves on rank 1 by 1.01 points [0.78, 1.27], and rank 3 adds 0.13 with an interval of [-0.32, 0.05]. These contrasts indicate that a second factor carries held-out predictive value on the ECI data, in line with the T2 rejection, even though no two-dimensional Bayesian candidate could be fitted and version families are not collapsed in that rerun. <!-- E-ECI-RANK -->

The third concerns the standard of evidence. BenchPress selects rank by held-out error and never tests adequacy. The Space numbers suggest that structure beyond one ability can be detectable and still be worth little in point accuracy. A rejection under Epoch's model and a small predictive gain from extra factors are therefore compatible.

The fourth concerns data. The BenchPress matrix and the ECI fitting set overlap little: 311 of Epoch's 2,879 cells have an accepted BenchPress row, covering 98 ECI models and 22 benchmarks, and 11 of the 57 ECI benchmarks appear in the May paper matrix. On those cells, 31.4% of rows agree exactly on the normalised scale and 77.5% agree within 0.05. The rank-2 finding in the paper therefore concerns a largely different set of benchmarks from ECI's. <!-- E-ECI-OVERLAP --> Neither paper offers a remedy for selection in which scores exist. Both evaluate on observed or uniformly masked cells, and the ECI fit conditions on the same kind of record. The review threads do not change this, since neither tests selection driven by publication, and the BenchPress authors' own shift results show interval coverage of 84.3% and 86.8% against a nominal 90% and a model mean that can beat BenchPress at one or two revealed scores on newer models (notes o5UgbYmeyg and qFhIqdhOhl). <!-- R-Z-o5UgbYmeyg --> <!-- R-Z-qFhIqdhOhl --> For imputing recent, sparsely observed models, this suggests that error figures from random masking are best read as optimistic for both methods, which is our inference.
